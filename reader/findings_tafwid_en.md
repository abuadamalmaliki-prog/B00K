# Was al-Saffarini a "mufawwid"? What 50 readers found in his book

## 1. The background

**The author.** Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Saffarini was a Hanbali scholar from
Nablus in Palestine. He died in 1188 AH (1774 CE).

**The book.** *Lawami' al-Anwar al-Bahiyya* ("The Gleaming Lights") is his
own commentary on a poem he wrote about Islamic creed, called *al-Durra
al-Mudiyya* ("The Shining Pearl"). The printed edition used here is Damascus
1982, in 2 volumes and 921 pages.

**The question.** The Qur'an and hadith describe God with words such as
"hand", "face", "eye", "He rose over the Throne" (*istiwa'*), "He descends
to the lowest heaven" (*nuzul*), mercy, love and anger. Scholars disagree
about how to treat these descriptions, and there are three main positions:

1. **Ta'wil (reinterpretation).** The words are figurative. "Hand" means
   power or blessing, "rose over the Throne" means took control, and so on.
2. **Tafwid al-ma'na (consigning the meaning).** We say the words and
   believe them, but we do not know what they mean at all. The meaning is
   left entirely to God. Someone who holds this is a *mufawwid*.
3. **Ithbat al-ma'na (affirming the meaning).** The words have a real,
   known meaning. "Hand" really is a hand-attribute, not power or blessing.
   What we don't know is *how* it is: its true nature and modality, called
   its *kayf* or *kunh*. This is summed up in Imam Malik's famous saying:
   "The rising is known; the *how* is unknown." Someone who holds this is a
   *muthbit al-ma'na*. It is sometimes also described as "consigning only
   the how".

**Why al-Saffarini is disputed.** In several places he uses phrases that
sound like position 2, such as "we leave its meaning to God" and "don't
delve into their meanings". Some people therefore call him a mufawwid.
Others say he holds position 3, and that by "meaning" in those phrases he
means the hidden reality, not the plain sense of the word.

## 2. How the book was read

- The whole book (921 pages) was split into 50 parts of about 16–24 pages
  each. Each part overlapped one page with its neighbours, so no argument
  was cut in half.
- 50 separate AI readers each read one part, every line of it.
- Every reader got the same neutral instructions:
  - collect every passage relevant to the question, quoted word for word,
    with its page number;
  - say who is speaking: al-Saffarini himself, his poem, or another scholar
    he is quoting (and whether he agrees with that scholar);
  - report evidence for **both** positions equally.
- The readers were not told which answer was expected.
- All 50 confirmed they read their part to the last line. Together they
  produced 456 pieces of evidence.

## 3. The verdict

**Al-Saffarini affirms the meaning of God's attributes and consigns only
their "how" (their true nature) to God. He is a muthbit al-ma'na, not a
mufawwid.**

This rests on clear statements in his own words, or in quotations he
explicitly approves. It does not depend on reading between the lines.

## 4. The decisive evidence

Page numbers are volume 1 unless marked "Vol. 2". The Arabic originals are
in the appendix, numbered to match.

**[1] Vol. 1, p. 25 — his own words.** He rejects the idea that the early
Muslims (the *salaf*) only believed in the words without understanding them:

> "These people went wrong because they thought the way of the salaf was
> merely believing in the words of the Qur'an and hadith without
> understanding them, like illiterate people … They have lied about the
> way of the salaf."

This describes position 2 exactly, and he calls it a lie against the salaf.

**[2] Vol. 1, pp. 116–117 — his own section heading, plus Ibn Taymiyya,
whom he approves.** He lists the "people of making-ignorant" (*ahl
al-tajhil*) among the groups who deviated from the truth. He defines them
like this:

> "Those who say the Messenger did not know the meanings of the verses
> about the attributes that were revealed to him … and that he spoke words
> whose meaning he did not know."

He then quotes Ibn Taymiyya to say what "the interpretation only God knows"
actually refers to:

> "It is the unknown *how*, about which the salaf such as Malik said: the
> rising is known, and the how is unknown."

So the thing only God knows is the *how*, not the meaning.

**[3] Vol. 1, p. 128 — his own summary.** Holding that God's names are
empty words is one of the worst deviations:

> "Stripping the names of their meanings and denying their realities, as
> the Jahmiyya say, that God's names are bare words containing no
> attributes and no meanings — this is among the greatest deviations
> regarding them, by reason, language, revelation and natural sense."

**[4] Vol. 1, p. 194 — his own words.** Ibn Taymiyya argues that the verses
saying God is above His creation mean exactly what they appear to say.
According to that argument, if God had meant something figurative, the
Prophet would have had to signal it. Al-Saffarini's comment:

> "This is solid reasoning that no one can escape."

**[5] Vol. 1, p. 211 — al-Wasiti, quoted with approval.** Refusing to say
anything about the meaning is condemned:

> "Holding back on this is ignorance and error. The Lord described Himself
> with these attributes so that we would know Him by them … So whoever God
> guides to affirming them, without distorting, without asking how, and
> without holding back, has reached what is required."

**[6] Vol. 1, p. 247 — his own words.** God's descent, His being above, and
His rising over the Throne are known; only their *how* is not:

> "All of that is established and known, without any how … because His
> essence and attributes are known in general terms: affirmed and known to
> exist, without a how and without limits."

**[7] Vol. 1, pp. 265–266 — his own words, inside an approved Ibn Taymiyya
passage.** Ibn Taymiyya lists six positions on the attribute verses.
Al-Saffarini follows his conclusion that the correct one is to take them on
their apparent sense, in the way that befits God:

> "The correct view, for most of the attributes, is decisively the second
> way."

The position "God knows best what He meant by it" is listed as one of the
positions that depart from the apparent meaning. Then he adds, in his own
words:

> "Only He knows the ultimate essence and reality of His attributes. The
> essence and the attributes are known only in general terms, in the way
> that befits His greatness."

**[8] Vol. 2, p. 468 — his own words, near the end of the book.** He
describes the later scholars' reinterpretation as:

> "Turning the revealed verses away from their established implication and
> their apparent meaning."

## 5. He explains what the attributes mean

Someone who consigned the meaning would not say what the attributes mean.
Al-Saffarini does so repeatedly:

- **Rising over the Throne.** "He rose over His Throne, above His seven
  heavens, … separate from His creation" (p. 190). He also quotes with
  approval: "It is the rising of the Essence over the Throne" (p. 196). He
  insists it does not mean "took control" (pp. 197–198).
- **Mercy.** "An eternal attribute subsisting in His essence, which entails
  favour and blessing" (pp. 32–33, 221). It is not "softness of the heart",
  which is human mercy, and it is not reducible to blessings God creates
  (p. 100). In his words: "Just as we affirm an essence unlike other
  essences, let us affirm a mercy unlike creatures' mercy" (p. 33).
- **The two hands.** "Two attributes of the essence, called hands, which
  are something more than blessing and power" (pp. 231–232).
- **The face.** An attribute in addition to the essence, not just another
  word for "God Himself" (pp. 226–228).
- **Hearing and sight.** Real attributes, distinct from knowledge, because
  of "the apparent meaning of the verses and hadiths" (pp. 143–144).
- **Speech.** God speaks with letters and sound. Quoting Ibn Taymiyya with
  approval: "A call can only be an audible voice, … neither literally nor
  figuratively anything else" (pp. 138–143, 169).
- **What "reinterpretation" means.** "Turning the word away from its
  apparent sense … or from its literal meaning to a figurative one" is
  "among the things condemned" (p. 102).

## 6. The passages that sound like tafwid, and why they don't contradict this

These are the passages people quote to call him a mufawwid. In each case
the qualification is right next to the phrase:

| Page | Phrase that sounds like tafwid | What sits beside it |
|---|---|---|
| pp. 97–99 | "Leaving its knowledge to God"; Ibn 'Abbas: "it is not explained"; Sufyan: "its explanation is reciting it and staying silent" | Same sentence: "a person must believe in its **apparent meaning**, and leave its knowledge to God." |
| p. 107 | "We leave its meaning to the Mighty, the Wise" | Same sentence: "and we do **not turn it away from the reality of His attribute**," and "affirmed in the way it came." |
| p. 219 | "Consigning their intended meaning to God" | Next lines: "the imams of the salaf held to **believing in the apparent meaning** of the attribute verses." |
| p. 241 | Imam Ahmad: "passed on as they came, without investigating their meanings" — "this is the truth" | The same quote says what is ruled out: "everything the mind **imagines and pictures** is giving it a how and likening it to creation." |
| p. 261 | "Don't engage with their meanings … that is beyond human capacity" | Comes right after "affirming existence, not affirming a how or limits." The *reality* of an attribute is beyond human capacity. The ordinary Arabic sense of "hand" or "rose" is not. |

**The key to his vocabulary.** In these phrases, al-Saffarini uses
"meaning" to mean the hidden reality of the attribute, not its plain sense.
He says so himself:

- p. 113: what cannot be known is "the reality of His essence and
  attributes … the mind cannot know the ultimate nature (*kunh*) of His
  essence."
- p. 215: "what His names and attributes signify, **which is their
  reality**, no one knows but Him."
- p. 266: what only God knows is "their ultimate essence and reality."

Read that way, every tafwid-sounding phrase fits with the passages in
section 4. Read the other way, those phrases would contradict his explicit
rejection of position 2 on p. 25.

## 7. Quotations that are not his own view

The book quotes many scholars. Some quotations support tafwid, but he only
reports them, and he answers each one with another quotation nearby:

| Page | Quotation | Answered by |
|---|---|---|
| p. 141 | Ibn Hajar: "either tafwid or ta'wil" | — (quoted while proving that God's voice is real) |
| p. 242 | al-Bayhaqi: "tafwid is safer" | al-Tufi, p. 243: "the words of the salaf indicate **affirming the meaning** that is disputed"; "the descent is **understood**, the how is unknown … and so with all the attributes." |
| p. 250 | al-Qadi Abu Ya'la: "a descent whose meaning is not understood" | al-Wasiti, p. 250: "His attributes are known in general and in their affirmation, but not understood in their how." |
| pp. 233, 236 | al-Nawawi: "their apparent meaning is not intended" | Ibn Taymiyya, pp. 237–238: the true apparent meaning is not likening God to creatures, and the texts indicate only what is true. |

## 8. What remains open

His wording on pp. 219, 241 and 261 is loose. Taken alone, those sentences
sound like tafwid. Taken with the rest of the book, they cannot mean
consigning the plain sense, because he explicitly calls that position a lie
against the salaf (p. 25) and a deviation (pp. 117, 128, 211).

The edition says it includes notes by two later scholars, 'Abdullah Aba
Butayn and Sulayman ibn Sahman. In this digital text their notes are not
marked separately, so it is not possible to check whether they commented on
these phrases.

## 9. What this shows about search tools ("RAG")

A keyword search for "consigning its meaning" returns exactly the
tafwid-sounding passages: pp. 219, 97 and 241, plus the Ibn Hajar and
al-Bayhaqi quotes. Two of those five are other people's words. None of the
decisive passages in section 4 contains those keywords. A reader who saw
only the search results would get the answer backwards. The right answer
only appeared from reading the whole book and keeping track of who was
speaking.

## 10. Where the evidence is in the book

- **Vol. 1, pp. 1–290:** method, God's names and attributes. Most of the
  evidence is here: 384 of the 456 items.
- **Vol. 1, pp. 291–470:** predestination, sins, faith. Indirect evidence
  only, for example that God's love and pleasure are distinct from His will
  (pp. 307, 328, 361).
- **Vol. 2:** the afterlife, prophethood, the Companions, logic, and the
  conclusion.
  - Seven parts have nothing on this topic.
  - He uses the same method elsewhere: things like the pens of destiny
    (Vol. 2, p. 288), the bridge (p. 192) and the scale (p. 184) are taken
    on their apparent sense, and only their *how* is left to God.
  - On seeing God in the hereafter: "He is seen but not encompassed, just
    as He is known but not comprehended" (Vol. 2, p. 247).

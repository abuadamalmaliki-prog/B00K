# Notes — Volume 3 (al-Juzʾ al-Thālith): the positive attributes
God is powerful, knowing, living, hearing, seeing, speaking, enduring, wise. Power must be preceded by defining "the powerful", which is
bound up with motives (dawāʿī) and deterrents (ṣawārif).

## Bāb 1: the rules of motives and deterrents

### L7584–7630 F1: definition of "the powerful" (al-qādir)
- "The best said: **one from whom it is valid to act at one time and not act at another, according to differing motives.**" A motive
  (dāʿī) = knowing, opining or believing that an act contains preponderant benefit → a firm inclination → the act (if limbs are sound);
  a deterrent is the converse. *Gharaḍ* = the benefit itself; dāʿī = the cognition of it; philosophers call it the final cause, also
  "the complete cause".
- What produces the act? (a) power + motive together; (b) power conditioned by motive; (c) the motive completes the power into a complete
  cause ("the final cause is the cause of the efficient cause's causality"); (d) power + motive yield complete *preparedness*, and the
  act itself comes "from higher causes". Razi: **"the nearest is that the cause is the conjunction of power and motive"** — power alone
  cannot act (shown later: no act without a motive), motive alone cannot; "power" = the limbs' being such that, with a motive, the act
  follows; for positive factors "part of the cause" vs "condition" makes no difference. "This alone does not settle which of the four is
  true." The philosophers' version (power + motive → preparedness, then the act emanates from the **Active Intellect**) "should be
  examined".
- ★ **The definition is problematic**: "not acting" = remaining in prior non-existence; non-existence cannot be the work of an agent, and
  the enduring cannot be ascribed to an agent → the powerful has no power over *refraining*. "This has further depth." Reply (some):
  the able man feels he could refrain if he willed, the incapable doesn't — known by necessity. **Razi: weak** — refraining is either
  something beyond continuing non-existence (doing a contrary; then one is able to do a thing and its contrary, not to "refrain"; God's
  eternal refraining from the world would be an eternal act of its contrary, and an eternal contrary could never cease, so the world
  could never exist; and the refrainer feels he did nothing) or it *is* continuing non-existence (not the work of an agent). → "**The
  definition 'the powerful is one from whom act and refraining are valid according to differing motives' is false.**"

### L7634–7682 F2: motives and deterrents
(1) Motives are *judgments* (taṣdīqāt), not concepts. (2) A deterrent is a motive to refrain. (3) Opinion and belief work like knowledge
(a lion on the road; **a Jew believes his rites bring great reward, a Muslim believes the same acts bring great harm** — each belief
moves). (4) Strength: a firm belief equals knowledge *while* firm; others: knowledge is stronger (belief can be shaken; the soul rests
more securely in truth); wavering belief is weaker; the false believer or imitator sometimes acts more boldly because of concomitants —
praise, worldly gain, long habit. Opinion with signs acts like knowledge but weaker, with degrees (many reporters > one; seeing fire near
one's house > being told of it); opinion vs belief varies.

### L7686–7730 F3: nothing but cognition moves to action
(1) The act's *actual* benefit or harm moves no one — the belief does (a false belief moves, an unknown benefit doesn't); and the goal
comes at the end ("the first in thought is the last in action") and cannot cause what precedes it; circular. (2) "God's promise moves,
His threat deters" — metaphor: the knowledge of benefit/harm in them moves. (3) Doubt — it is equipoise; what moves to inquiry is the
knowledge that inquiry will avert a possible harm. (4) Seeing and hearing move via the knowledge they give. (5) Will/aversion,
desire/repulsion: some say will *is* knowledge of benefit; others that inclination *follows* that knowledge. Example: a man going to visit
a friend remembers business at home; if the benefits seem equal he stands still; once one preponderates he moves → **inclination and will
follow beliefs**.

### L7734–7815+ F4: benefit and harm, good and evil, welfare and corruption
- There must be things desired for themselves (else regress or circle): **pleasure (ladhdha) and joy (surūr)**; hated for themselves:
  **pain (alam) and grief (ghamm)**. Means are desired/hated for others. *Khayr/maṣlaḥa* covers what is wanted for itself or another;
  *sharr/mafsada* what is hated for itself or another. Praise = words indicating perfection → joy; blame → grief — so they fall under
  the same scheme.
- Divisions: (1) hated per se: harm, loss of benefit, removal of what removes harm; desired per se: benefit, removal of harm, removal of
  what removes benefit. Harm is worse than loss of an equal benefit (the view that loss of present benefits equals harm is weak — compare
  equal quantities); removing harm is more urgent than gaining benefit ("averting harm from oneself is obligatory, gaining benefit is not").
  (2) Whether pleasure or joy, pain or grief, is stronger varies with souls — no firm judgment. (3) The per se outweighs the per accidens
  unless the latter is much greater.
- Second division: what is believed purely beneficial → the act is necessary, the agent "as if compelled (ka-l-muljaʾ)" — the thirsty
  faster in summer, water present, Law and doctor saying drink; what is believed purely harmful → refraining is necessary — entering fire …
  … (a man may kill himself or leap from a cliff only when he believes a greater pain is otherwise inescapable — still "averting harm").
  Mixed cases: preponderant benefit → the act is necessary (equal parts cancel; preferring the outweighed is impossible); preponderant
  harm → refraining; equal → things stay as they are; no belief either way → no motive, no change.
- **Branches**: (1) *Muʿtazila*: obligation (taklīf) is invalid under a pure motive (iljāʾ, compulsion) and valid only when motives
  "waver". ★ **Razi: "very weak"** — in every case one side is necessary; "wavering" only means a motive to act at one moment and to
  refrain at the next, i.e. compulsion each moment. (2) Beliefs may persist or flicker → the "fickle man" (dhū badawāt); the relevant
  considerations of welfare are beyond count — the more one sees of them, the more perplexity. (3) ★ **Belief in preponderant harm has
  no effect on refraining**: refraining is just the original non-existence persisting ("the cause of non-existence is the
  non-existence of the cause"); so **neither power nor motive affects refraining — only the act's existence is ascribable to the
  agent.**
- *Third division*: grades of benefit and harm are innumerable — (1) pleasure/joy, pain/grief vary in intensity without limit; (2) the
  same thing is delightful to one, indifferent to another, repugnant to a third; (3) per se vs per accidens; (4) the per accidens can
  outweigh (finding dinars vs eating a morsel); (5) the number of intermediaries; (6) one thing good in one respect, bad in another;
  (7) endless mixtures; (8) remoteness vs relation; (9) persistence breeds love in some, tedium in others; (10) pleasant by one route,
  odious by another. And since motives are *beliefs*, thoughts shift without bound, **rational souls differ in quiddity**, and celestial
  factors intervene → **no two people follow one path, nor does one person keep one path** — hence the variety of animal acts.

### L7940–7996 F5: further branches
(1) Can an act be beneficial in every respect? Bodily acts always involve fatigue (motion → heat → weakness); **purely spiritual acts are
pure benefit — one reason spiritual pleasures surpass bodily ones**. An act harmful in every respect is impossible: any willed act is
pleasant to its agent. (2) One act can combine many independent motives. (3) ★ **Overdetermination**: if several concurring motives are
each sufficient, the act occurs by each (two sufficient causes of one effect — impossible), by one (unpreponderated), or by none (then a
strongly motivated, unhindered agent could not act) — "a strong question". Conflicting motives: the stronger prevails; equal ones cancel.
(4) Motives must coexist with the act if power + motive is the *efficient* cause; precede it if only a *preparatory* cause.

### L8000–8112 F6: does action depend on a motive?
- **Yes**: the verifying philosophers and Abū l-Ḥusayn al-Baṣrī; **no**: most mutakallimūn.
- *"No" arguments*: (1) a powerful agent may lack all relevant beliefs and still act (we move a finger without knowing how many parts
  cross how many places; people dispute which kinds of thing are within human power); "powerful = able to act" — if he couldn't act
  without beliefs he'd be powerful and not powerful; (2) the absent-minded and sleepers act (fidgeting finger; the archer's miss that
  hits another; the sleeper breathes, turns, talks); (3) the skilled scribe or lute-player doesn't attend to each letter or stroke;
  (4) two cups, two loaves, two roads; (5) a slow motion contains interspersed rests (atomists) or a quality of slowness, of which the
  agent is unaware; (6) dependence on motive erases the self-evident difference between a necessitating cause and a free agent.
- *Replies*: (1) "able to act by mere power" is the point in dispute; "able given a motive" is granted — like arguing a possible "can"
  exist by itself without any cause; (2) the fidgeter does intend, for some pleasure (relief from a tedious posture, habit, a passing
  fancy); the sleeper's dream-images act as wills, long lying on one side hurts, breathing is quasi-necessary; **Avicenna**: imagining,
  being aware of imagining, and remembering it are three things — lacking the memory does not show lack of the first two; (3) habit
  (malaka) forms in the fingers and in the imagination, whose images succeed so fast they seem absent; (4) there is always some
  preponderance (the nearer hand); if absolutely none, **"God creates in his heart a necessary, decisive motive"**; (5) the slow mover
  knows he moves slowly; (6) the difference between the powerful and the necessitating cause will be given.

### L8120–8245+ F7: arguments that action requires a motive
1. Power relates equally to acting and refraining; preponderance without a preponderator is self-evidently impossible. "Begging the
   question"? — **"the powerful preponderates without a preponderator" is self-contradictory**: "preponderates" adds something beyond
   power, "without a preponderator" denies it; if it adds nothing, delete it, and you have "power relates equally to both, and one
   happens" — evidently false.
2. Powerful for a time without acting, then acting: either because he *made it occur* then (an extra element — the motive) or with no
   extra element — then it happened by itself, severing act from agent. "This motion's occurring by this agent" is a notion distinct
   from the motion and from power (power existed earlier without it).
3. **Possibility is one notion**: as with an atom's location and a possible's existence, so with power's two sides. Opponents'
   difference: making the agent's choice depend on something else turns him into a necessitator, while the difference between qādir
   and mūjib is known (praise, blame, reward, punishment). *Reply*: possibility per se grounds the need; "unless it leads to an
   absurdity" can never be applied, since we can never know that *no* absurdity follows ("not knowing ≠ knowing it is not") — that
   qualification would destroy the principle altogether.
4. If power alone sufficed, motives would never make a difference (like a stone's colour in its falling); but they plainly do (we give
   a dirham to a poor man *because* we know he is poor).
5. Introspection: the man between visiting his friend and returning home stands still until one side tips; the man offered two cups
   pauses to decide; in expiations (kaffārāt) the believer chooses what is easiest.
6. If acts didn't need motives: God would deserve no praise (He might have created benefits with no benevolent motive); miracles
   would not prove a prophet's truthfulness; speech would not indicate meaning … ("the default is that things stay as they were") …
   (and similarly miracles would not prove God's intent to confirm the prophet, since He might create them with no motive).
- The opponents' main reply — "acting without a motive is possible only for an ignorant agent; a knower acts only from a motive" — is
  false: knowledge does not change the nature of power; and they themselves allow preference without preponderator for knowers (two
  cups; the moment of creation; each atom's place).

### L8274–8372 F8: once the motive is present, the act is *necessary* (not merely "more fitting")
- Proofs: (1) under equality the act is impossible; once one side preponderates the other is outweighed, which is worse than equal →
  impossible → the first is necessary (objection: refraining is persisting non-existence, untouched by the motive, so non-existence too
  stays "preponderant" by default — reply: if being preponderates, non-being is outweighed relative to it). (2) If non-existence were
  still not impossible, suppose the act sometimes follows and sometimes not; the difference needs an extra factor (then before it the
  act was impossible) or not (unpreponderated) — "a decisive proof". (3) Under equality there is neither requirer nor preventer and the
  act is still impossible; with a motive there is no requirer for the other side *and* a preventer → a fortiori impossible. (4) Refraining
  in the presence of a motive to act would be by a counter-motive (they'd cancel) or by none (preponderance without motive).
- Opponents: (1) one can change one's motive; when motives conflict one still chooses (the expiations); (2) then no difference between
  mūjib and mukhtār, and praise/blame, encouragement, command and prohibition would be pointless; (3) we know the difference between being
  compelled and choosing. *Answers*: (1) changeability doesn't mean the motive fails to necessitate while present; equal motives = the
  previous question; (2) two real differences: the chooser acts *knowing* he is the source; and a natural cause has one fixed quality while
  the chooser's motives are fleeting — when they change, the act changes; "whoever tests himself knows this: when a pure, unopposed motive
  arises in his heart he is as if compelled to act".
- ★ **"A scholar said something fine"**: someone says "I find that if I will, I act; if I will, I refrain." — "Grant it; but do you find that
  if you will to will, the will occurs? That regresses; reason is certain the chain ends in a motive that falls into your heart from no
  other motive, and once it falls you act necessarily. **Neither the motive's arising nor the act's following it is from you — man is
  compelled in the form of a chooser (al-insān muḍṭarr fī ṣūrat mukhtār).**" Command, reward and blame are answered in the question of
  the creation of acts (vol. 9).

### L8378–8432 F9: divisions of motives
(1) Some motives the servant produces (we can, by effort, remove a desire from our heart); some God creates directly — the first ones,
otherwise regress → **every chain of heart-states rises to a necessary motive created by God, with each later motive following
necessarily**. (2) Universal vs particular motives: the will to visit a friend is universal over many routes; it yields an act only with a
particular motive (this route); if there is only one route, the universal will + that knowledge necessitate the particular will ("what is
necessary to the wanted is wanted"). The universal will persists through the walk and produces a succession of particular wills, one per
step, each conditioned on the lapse of the previous.

### L8436–8566 F10: motive of need vs motive of benevolence (iḥsān)
- *Muʿtazilī scheme*: the motive to bring benefit to oneself = **need**; to others = **benevolence/wisdom**, grounded in a quality of the
  *act* (its being good in itself) rather than of the agent. Ugliness compels refraining; goodness invites without compelling; added
  priority strengthens it; obligation makes it strongest.
- *Philosophers and mutakallimūn who deny rational good/evil* reject the benevolence motive: (1) good/evil = conducing to preponderant
  benefit/harm; God is beyond benefit and harm → no such motive in Him. *Muʿtazila*: good ≠ beneficial: oppression benefits the oppressor
  yet his own reason calls it ugly; a fine poem, beautifully written and sung, that insults angels and prophets is pleasant yet ugly; a
  useful lie is ugly; **helping a blind, dying man in an empty desert — even by a Dahrī who expects no reward or praise — is good with no
  benefit**. *Replies*: the oppressor who approved oppression could not defend himself against it; licensing insult would destroy the
  standing of God's command and of the greatest benefactor; licensing lies destroys trust; and the desert case: man projects others' states
  onto himself, so helping relieves the pain of **"species-pity" (riqqat al-jinsiyya)**, plus a social convention of benevolence (in hope of
  reciprocity) internalised from childhood → **good and evil reduce to welfare**. (2) Even if they differed, experience shows only the
  need-motive: the Dahrī helper seeks to be praiseworthy in his own eyes, to relieve his pity, and the rare case is assimilated to the
  usual praised one; remove all three and "we do not know he would help".
- *Muʿtazilī proof*: the world was made by a non-body who cannot need; acting without motive is a defect → He acted from benevolence.
  *"The sages" reply*: (1) acts need motives; human motives end in God-created ones and acts follow necessarily → **human acts are effects
  of God's acts; whoever creates the cause creates and wills the effect → evils occur by His will and decree** → the dependence of His acts
  on benevolence fails; (2) whoever acts for a purpose would be deficient without it, perfected by another — impossible for God ("for
  others' benefit": if their benefit and its absence are equal for Him He cannot want it; if not, He is perfected by it); (3) the world's
  specific time and place among infinitely many equal ones were chosen either for a special wisdom (regress) or for none (acting without a
  preponderating motive). "**Full proofs that God's acts and rulings are not explained by interests and purposes (taʿlīl afʿāl Allāh
  bi-l-maṣāliḥ wa-l-aghrāḍ) will come.**"

### L8570–8584 F11: how the servant is an agent — Razi's doctrine
★ "**What we hold**: the conjunction of power and a specific motive necessitates (mustalzim) the act" — "necessitates" being common to a
preparatory and an efficient cause. Since God creates both power and motive, and their conjunction necessitates the act, **the servant is
truly the agent (his power and motive are what produce the act), and all servants' acts occur by God's decree — not a mote in heaven or
earth escapes the chain of His decree and measuring**. Proof: preponderance needs a preponderator; that preponderator is God's act (else
regress); given it, the preponderance is necessary. Full treatment in its own book.

## Bāb 2: the difference between the powerful (qādir) and the necessitating cause (mūjib)
### L8592–8695+ The philosophers' case against "acting by validity, not necessity"
- Religious communities: an agent either affects with the possibility of not affecting (qādir) or not (mūjib).
- *Philosophers*: (1) if everything needed for the effect is present (time, interest, absence of obstacles, instruments — every condition,
  positive or negative), the effect is necessary (else its occurring at one time rather than another needs a further factor, or is
  unpreponderated); if anything is missing, it is impossible; no middle → "acting by way of validity (ṣiḥḥa)" is unintelligible.
  Mutakallimūn have only two replies — (a) with a preponderator the act becomes more fitting, not necessary; (b) the chooser can prefer
  without a preponderator (cups, loaves, roads), otherwise qādir and mūjib would not differ — "**no third reply exists**", and both were
  examined above. (2) **God knows everything**: what He knows will occur is necessary, what He knows won't is impossible (knowledge must
  match its object) → His power acts by necessity or impossibility. Objection: knowledge follows its object as it is, i.e. as contingent.
  Reply: why can't a thing be possible in itself and necessary through its causes (knowledge and power)? (3) An *eternal* world is either
  necessary in itself (then no cause at all) or possible and caused — not by a chooser (that would be producing the existent) → by a mūjib;
  an *originated* world: if its cause is power, that power is originated (regress) or eternal — then its attachment to producing that event
  at that time is eternal and cannot cease ("what is eternal cannot cease") → necessary, not contingent; the same holds of will. (4) The
  "validity" of acting obtains before, at, or after the effect; "at t1 He can produce at t2" is impossible, since producing at t2 needs t2
  present …
  … ; "at the time of the act" is impossible (when it exists its non-existence is impossible); "after" is absurd. (5) God's power's
  attachment to producing the world "in non-eternity": by validity → needs a preponderator, the will → the will's attachment: by validity
  (regress) or necessity (then He is a mūjib); "valid yet needing no preponderator" would mean contingency needs no cause — destroying the
  proof of a Maker. (6) Power over refraining is impossible (refraining = persisting non-existence) → the qādir relates only to the side of
  existence → no difference from a mūjib; the "felt difference" reply is weak — the felt difference concerns only the existence side (the
  able man produces when a motive comes, the incapable doesn't). (7) The cause of all possibles is not possible → the respect in which it
  causes is necessary → it causes necessarily.
- **Religious communities' six arguments that God is a chooser**: (1) a mūjib is unconditioned (then its effect is co-eternal) or
  conditioned by an eternal condition (same) or an originated one (regress, simultaneous or beginningless — both refuted). *Objection*: if
  the world was possible in eternity, its eternity is not impossible; if impossible, an eternal cause may still not produce it (the
  recipient's possibility counts). *Replies*: (a) an effect lagging its cause is impossible and the world's eternity is impossible → the
  cause is not a mūjib; (b) even if azal blocks it, the block lifts in non-eternity → the world should arise at the first moment after —
  but every such moment has an earlier one → it should always have arisen earlier → absurd. (2) Tamāthul al-ajsām → specification of
  bodies' attributes needs a non-bodily cause that relates equally to all → it must be a chooser. (Both depend on rejecting beginningless
  events.) (3) A mūjib's effect is one (then a chain of single effects, so of any two motes one would cause the other — absurd) or many
  (against "from one only one"; or composition). *Objections*: (i) **the Avicennian triad** — from the first effect's possibility comes the
  matter of the outermost sphere, from its existence its form, from its necessity-through-another the intellect that governs it; (ii) the
  First's effect is just *existence*, diversified by receptacles (as one sunlight falls variously on stone and tree); (iii) why not many
  from one? *Razi's twelve answers to (i)*: possibility is not an existent, so causes nothing; if existent it is a second effect of the
  First; possibility is one species, so every possibility would cause such a matter; the matter's own possibility would then cause its
  existence → a self-necessitating quiddity; philosophers deny one thing is receiver and agent, yet possibility = receptivity; the sphere
  is divisible, so composite of many parts from one possibility; existences are one species, so every existence would cause the sphere's
  form; existence causing without the quiddity would make it self-standing; necessity-through-another is either non-existent (no cause) or a
  third effect of the First (and would need its own necessity → regress); the sphere comprises matter, corporeal form, specific form,
  magnitude, shape, position — something from each category → many from three; the first intellect knows itself and all its effects → many
  forms → many effects. *To (ii)*: if the First caused quiddities, their removal would change them — equally true of existence; and
  existences are many (one accident cannot be in many loci) → many effects. *To (iii)*: you yourselves accept the principle; we used your
  own proof. (4) A mūjib's effects' removal implies God's removal (backward chain) — "reveals" rather than "causes" changes nothing.
  (5) A mūjib → an eternal world → an eternal that needs a cause → the enduring needing a cause (impossible). (6) Many eternals are impossible:
  eternity = not-being-preceded-by-non-existence (negation of a negation → positive) → eternals share it and differ → composite → infinite
  composition → no "one".
- **Philosophers' replies**: (1) the First may necessitate a *chooser* who makes the world; and "effect at some times only" is equally a
  problem for the qādir (unpreponderated timing); the world's possibility has no beginning (else impossible-in-itself became possible) → the
  world's eternity is not impossible. (2) Same problem for a qādir (no preference among equals without a preponderator). (3) ★ **Razi: "the
  nearest view is that one thing *can* be the cause of many; the philosophers' proofs of 'from one only one' are very weak — this is the
  verified, relied-upon position"**; their emanation scheme was refuted. (4) Power + motive together are a complete necessitating cause → all
  objections to the mūjib apply to the chooser. (5) Whether the enduring needs a cause — separate treatment. (6) Religious communities agree
  God was knowing and powerful from eternity and that these are not His essence → many eternals on their own view.

## Bāb 3: God is knowing (ʿālim)
### L9016–9068 F1: the nature of knowledge
Four views: (1) **mere relation (nisba/iḍāfa)** — "held by a great group of sages and mutakallimūn; **our chosen view, and the truth**":
knowing is a relation between mind and known, so there is no knowledge without a known facing the knower; (2) a real attribute = the
known's *form* in the knower — most philosophers (refuted "at the start of the logic of this book"); (3) a real attribute with a relation
(taʿalluq) to its object, not a likeness of it — most mutakallimūn; (4) negative: some early mutakallimūn ("knowledge = absence of
ignorance" — muddled: if ignorance = absence of knowledge, knowledge = absence of absence = positive; if false belief, stones lack it too);
philosophers' "to be immaterial is to be intellect" — unclear (matter has no further matter yet knows nothing). Razi's charitable
reconstruction: knowledge = presence of the known's form to the knower; what exists in a substrate is present to another, not to itself;
what is self-subsistent is present to itself → it knows itself.

### L9074–9109+ F2: the mutakallimūn's proof that God knows — precision and mastery (iḥkām, itqān)
God's acts are precise and masterly (conforming to welfare — the wisdom in heavens, elements, meteorology, minerals, plants, animals, the
human body; "this great, noble book is full of it"); whoever acts with precision knows (the ignorant cannot weave brocade or write well;
some call this self-evident). *Objections*: (1) ★ **"A clear question, and I have not seen any mutakallim deal with it; neglecting it in
such lofty matters is a wonder"**: these proofs show the world's *maker* knows and has power, not that the Necessary Being does — the maker
could be an effect of the Necessary, directly or through intermediaries. *Answers*: (a) the method of the more fitting (al-awlā wa-l-akhlaq):
accept the certain and drop the unproven intermediaries; (b) once God is shown powerful, prove there is only one agent in existence.
(2) What is "precision"? Conformity to welfare in some respect — the ignorant achieve that too; in every respect — who can show it?
(we do not know whether a larger or smaller sun, other celestial arrangements, or another human form would be better; the alternatives
are infinite) …
- (3) Does precision imply knowledge? (a) the ignorant sometimes produce one precise act (a line of verse, one well-formed letter); "like
  has the like's judgment" → in principle always; (b) **bees build perfect hexagons without ruler or compass (which wise men cannot),
  spiders build marvels, ants split grains so they won't sprout** — if precision implied knowledge, animals would be wiser than men;
  (c) the master scribe writes best while thinking of something else; deliberation may even hinder; (d) philosophers ascribe the forming of
  bodies to unconscious nature, and people praise a perfected craft as "become nature"; (e) **the mutakallimūn's own answer to the
  time/place question** — a chooser may specify without a preponderator — means mere power could produce this welfare-conforming
  arrangement without knowledge. (4) Opinion might suffice: artisans work by conjecture (no scribe has certainty about pen-tip, paper,
  ink, finger motions) — and the world's defects might come from a conjecturing maker. (5) **The world is full of evils** — blindness,
  chronic illness, crushing poverty; "**the man perfect in knowledge and practice deprived and humiliated under the ignorant and base,
  while the lowest of creatures dominate**"; either God made the world for wisdom (then the evils show a lack of knowledge or power) or by
  mere power (then its fitness was not intended and power suffices). The reply "evils don't prove ignorance — a knower can do bad acts"
  misses the point: reason says precision implies knowledge, and equally that one who is powerful, knowing and merciful would not inflict
  pain on the weak; if you can override the second judgment, others can override the first. (6) **On al-Ashʿarī's principles the
  argument is unavailable**: for him structure (binya) is no condition of life, knowledge, power — a single atom could be the most knowing
  of creatures; organs work only by God's custom (ʿāda); so "the eye is made for sight, the stomach for digestion" — the anatomists'
  "uses of the parts" — has no basis, and with it "precision" vanishes.

### L9214–9236 F3: a stronger route from God's being a free chooser
(a) *Conception*: a chooser acts by intending; intending presupposes conceiving the quiddities → God conceives them. (b) *Judgment*:
quiddities are mutually entailing, excluding, or neither; mediated entailments are chains of immediate ones, each due to the quiddity
itself → conceiving the quiddities yields knowing every relation step by step → everything is known. "**More precise and further from
objections.**" Variant: to intend existence He must conceive "existent", which divides into necessary and possible → He knows both;
knowing a universal yields its division into particulars, and so on — "a good route, strengthened by the logical point that knowing a
quiddity yields knowing its proximate concomitant".

### L9240–9509+ F4: Avicenna's three routes to God's knowledge, examined
- Route 1 (knowing Himself → knowing all); Route 2 (knowing others → knowing Himself); Route 3 (an immaterial substance united with an
  immaterial form intellects; the immaterial-by-itself is all the more intellect, intellecter and intellected).
- **Route 1(a)** — He is self-subsistent and immaterial, hence knows Himself: knowledge = presence of the quiddity to the knower; what is
  self-subsistent is present to itself; we know ourselves not by a likeness (two likes; which would be subject?) but by the self's presence
  to itself. *Objections*: knowledge is not presence of a form (refuted); presence is at most a condition; presence is a relation needing
  two — replies: "presence to a thing" is wider than "to another" (Razi: very weak — it would allow self-moving and self-causing), an
  individual = quiddity + individuation + whole (some difference — "better"), people say "my self" (relation of self to self); and even
  granting it for us, our souls differ in quiddity from God.
- **Route 1(b)** — knowing Himself, He knows He causes everything, hence everything in descending order. *Razi*: (i) it works only for a
  necessitating cause, not for a chooser; (ii) "A is A" ≠ "A causes B" (tautology vs information; essence vs relation; each knowable
  without the other) → knowing the essence ≠ knowing its causality, unless "knowing a thing yields knowing its concomitants" — which is the
  conclusion → circular. Evidence that knowing a quiddity does *not* yield its concomitants: every quiddity stands in relations to all
  others, so knowing one would mean knowing infinitely many; we know body's quiddity intuitively but not whether it is eternal or
  originated; our soul is immaterial and self-present yet doesn't know intuitively that it is immaterial, originated or everlasting; the
  cause is a concomitant of the effect too, so knowing ourselves would reveal the higher causes. (Possible rejoinder: knowing a cause gives
  its effect, but knowing an effect gives only *some* cause, not this one — "a clear, reasonable difference".)
- **Route 2** (Ishārāt, namaṭ 3, on the earthly soul — editor quotes it): we can know God together with other things; knowing = an imprinted
  form; the co-presence of two forms in the mind is possible, and that possibility can't be conditioned on their being mental (else
  possibility would depend on actuality) → even in re God's essence can be accompanied by the forms of intelligibles, which *is*
  intellection; whatever is possible for God is necessary for Him (else change: His essence suffices for it, excludes it, or neither →
  dependence → possible) → He knows others; whoever knows can know that he knows → He knows Himself.
  *Objections*: (1) the philosophers' own thesis that God's essence is unknown to humans → we can't know Him together with anything;
  (2) the mind cannot attend to two things at once, and strong objects drown weak ones (**a lamp before the sun, a mosquito's whine in
  thunder**); "knowing the world depends on the Necessary" is knowing an attribute, not the essence; (3) induction can't cover infinitely
  many objects; (4) what holds of each needn't hold of all; (5) co-presence of objects ≠ of cognitions (imprint theory again); (6) **three
  species of co-presence** — two inherents in one locus, an inherent with its locus, a locus with its inherent — differ in quiddity: our
  mental co-presence of God's form with others is the first, soul-with-form the second, God-in-re-with-forms the third → no inference →
  "pure sophistry"; (7) a mental quiddity is an accident, an extra-mental one a substance; the argument needs our mental form of God to
  equal God in quiddity (making Him a species with individuals — false by agreement) …
  … (3) a mental image of the sky equal in quiddity to the sky would itself be a sky; (4) by the same parity, since the mental form of God
  needs a locus, God would need one; (5) since things outside the mind know, mental forms equal to them would know. — Q8: even granting
  co-presence, knowledge is not co-presence but a relation conditioned on it, possibly with further unmet conditions. Q9: possibility is
  not enough — a cause is required; if His essence does not require it, nothing else can → it stays absent although receivable.
  Q10: "knowing others → able to know that He knows → knows Himself": (a) we judge "this individual is human" — particular subject,
  universal predicate → one faculty grasping both, which Avicenna holds impossible (particulars are perceived bodily, universals
  immaterially); (b) the infinite orders of knowing-that-one-knows, all actual in God → infinitely many cognitions and causal chains at once.
- **Route 3** (*al-Mabdaʾ wa-l-maʿād*): the soul knows by receiving an immaterial form; a self-subsistent immaterial form would know all the
  more (as heat, were it self-subsistent, would heat all the more) — **"taken from the method of the more fitting; it yields only weak
  opinion."**
- Others: (1) God is the most perfect; knowledge is a perfection → He knows everything; (2) our knowledge comes from Him; a perfection in the
  effect is more fitting in the cause.

### L9572–9780 F5: questions branching from God's knowledge
1. *Does God know Himself?* Some deny: knowledge is a relation needing distinctness; our soul is composite (so relations are possible),
   God is simple; "knower/known" aspects are posterior to the knowing → circular. Razi affirms (He is immaterial and self-present; whoever
   knows can know he knows) and answers: **God's essence is a determinate essence — quiddity, individuation, and their sum — enough otherness
   for relations.**
2. *Avicenna: "He is intellect, intellecter and intellected, all one."* Razi: the three terms are synonyms (then pointless) or distinct —
   positive (knowledge isn't negative) attributes → plurality; knowledge is relational → not identical with the essence; they depend on one
   another in order → not one notion.
3. *The kalām proof that He knows everything*: He can know each object (being living, nothing bars any knowing); the cause of His knowings
   is His essence (knowing is a relation, so an attribute, so possible, so caused — by His essence); His essence relates equally to all →
   He knows all. *Objections*: "the living can know all" is either self-evident (then drop the proof) or unproven; His essence might
   necessitate some knowings only — "no more fitting" in reality is denied (knowings of different objects differ; mutakallimūn themselves
   make each knowing a distinct quiddity bound to its object). **"The better course: God is the most perfect, ignorance of anything is a
   defect → He knows all; and this is also the safer view (iḥtiyāṭ)."**
4. *Deniers of divine knowledge as such*: (1) His knowing is His essence (no: "the Necessary exists" is empty, "the Necessary knows" informs;
   their contradictories differ; one can be doubted while the other is known; a relation is not a self-subsistent essence) or other —
   inhering, needing a cause, i.e. His essence (one producing many; receiver and agent; and the eternal cannot be caused); (2) if knowledge
   is a perfection, God is perfected by another; if not, deny it; (3) knowledge is a relation, so God depends on the other relatum; (4) many
   objects → many knowings → infinite attributes → composite → possible; "attributes are not God" — then God is either essence + attributes
   (possible) or the bare essence (attributes extraneous → deny them). *Answers*: (1) rests on refuted principles (from one only one; not
   receiver and agent; need grounded in origination); (2) **His essence is perfect by itself, and among the concomitants of that perfection is
   that it necessitates knowledge**; (3) the same would refute His causing the world (causality too is a relation); (4) **unity is half of
   two, a third of three … — infinitely many relations do not impair essential unity.**

### L9784–9957+ F6: does God know particulars?
- The report that philosophers deny it "needs scrutiny": He knows His own determinate essence (a particular) and His first effect. Correctly:
  **they deny He knows changing things *qua* changing, and bodily things in their specific magnitudes.**
- *Argument 1 — Zayd sits, then stands*: the knowledge "Zayd is sitting" either persists (ignorance) or ceases (change in God). Two kalām
  camps: (a) **the knowledge stays, the object changes**: one knowledge covers many objects, so changing ones too; a mirror shows each
  passer-by without itself changing (editor objects to the analogy); the belief "Zayd will enter tomorrow", persisting, becomes "he is entering
  now"; knowledge is a real attribute whose *relation* changes (as one becomes "to the left" when a neighbour moves). (b) **the knowledge
  changes**: the man in a dark room who keeps believing "Zayd enters tomorrow" does not know Zayd is entering now unless he also knows it is
  tomorrow (a third knowledge is generated); "will exist" and "exists now" are different quiddities, and quiddities cannot turn into one
  another; one presupposes present existence, the other excludes it — like contraries; forms matching different quiddities differ — "near
  self-evident". *Razi's replies to (a)*: knowledge does multiply with objects (on either theory), and multiplicity ≠ change; **"in *Kitāb
  al-ʿIlm* we said knowledge is nothing but this unveiling; positing an attribute beyond the relation lacks proof — and if such an attribute
  necessitated the relation, the relation's removal would remove it"**; most mutakallimūn hold accidents (beliefs) do not endure, so how do they
  know what would happen if one did? and the dark-room case refutes them.
- *Camp (b) in detail* — **Abū l-Ḥusayn al-Baṣrī, Jahm b. Ṣafwān, Hishām b. al-Ḥakam**: His essence necessitates knowledge of a thing on
  condition of the thing's state; when that lapses, one knowledge ceases and another arises. Later mutakallimūn's objections: (1) a new
  knowledge needs a maker who knows it (precision) → regress — *weak*, since they say the essence necessitates it, not produces it by choice;
  (2) it would arise in God (making Him a locus of events), elsewhere, or in no locus — "locus of events" just restates the thesis; (3) the
  essence suffices for the attribute, for its absence, or neither (dependence → possible) — **"good" (jayyid)**; (4) the eternal knowledge "the
  world does not exist now" either persists (contradiction) or ceases — an eternal cannot cease, an originated one needs a prior knowledge
  (regress, beginningless events) or a first (prior ignorance). Jahm and Hishām: **the eternal knowledge is of quiddities (conceptions); the
  judgments "x now exists / has ceased" arise with the events.**
- *Philosophers' argument 2*: a square flanked by two equal squares — the two flanking squares are distinguished only if they exist; not
  externally (we can imagine them when they don't exist, and God must know before He creates) → mentally …
  … (cont. L9958) if the two flanking squares were in one mental substrate they would share quiddity, concomitants and receptivity to every
  accident equally, so nothing could distinguish them; yet they are distinguished → they must sit in two substrates differing by position
  (waḍʿ) and place → the perceiver of such forms is a body or bodily; God is neither → He cannot perceive them. *Razi's reply*: this rests on
  perception requiring the known to be *present* (a form) in the knower's mind — already refuted "in ways that leave a rational person no doubt".
- *Philosophers' argument 3*: knowledge "x exists / x does not exist" follows (tābiʿ) its object (a matching form depends on the object being
  so, not vice versa). If God's essence were necessarily qualified by such knowledges, His essence would depend on the knowledges, which
  depend on the contingent states of affairs → the Necessary would depend on possibles → possible in itself. Contrast: knowledge of quiddities
  holds whether or not they are realised, so the essence suffices for it. *Razi's reply*: the same reasoning would forbid His being an agent
  (taʾthīr) at all, since causing is a relation that is completed only by the other; "a difference can be drawn in some respects. God knows best."
  — End of the deniers' case.
- **Arguments FOR knowledge of particulars**: (1) His acts are precise and well-made (muḥkam mutqan), which requires knowledge of the act; and
  precision shows itself only in *individual* existents → the very proof of His knowing is a proof of His knowing particulars. (2) An
  individual has a quiddity plus an individuation (tashakhkhuṣ). If individuation = the quiddity, knowing the quiddity is knowing it; if it is
  additional, it is itself a possible quiddity — and the philosophers grant that **knowledge of the cause yields knowledge of the effect**, so
  His knowledge of His own essence yields knowledge of whatever produces this individuation → He knows the individual *as* that individual.
  (3) Knowledge is perfection, ignorance deficiency; He is the most perfect. (4) **The argument from fiṭra**: the sincere and the zindīq, the
  monotheist and the atheist, when struck by calamity all implore God to deliver them — even the fiercest denier of His knowing particulars.
  The primordial nature testifies that the God of the world is able and knows secrets, "and the testimony of the original fiṭra is closer to
  acceptance than these hidden divisions and obscure inquiries". ★ Razi's conjecture: **Abraham's words to his father, "why do you worship
  what neither hears nor sees nor avails you anything?" (Q19:42), were said because his father "was of the religion of the philosophers" and
  denied God's power and knowledge of particulars.** "This is what I have on this question. God knows best."

### L10020–10121 Bāb 3 F7: God knows non-existents (maʿdūmāt)
(MS (S) numbers it F6.)
- Among those who affirm knowledge of particulars, **Jahm and Hishām**: He knows particulars only once they exist; before that only
  quiddities. **The majority**: He knows them before they exist.
- This depends on whether the non-existent can be known. Proofs that it can: (1) we know the sun will rise tomorrow, which does not exist now;
  (2) a precise act presupposes the agent's knowledge, and the condition precedes the conditioned → His knowledge of His acts precedes their
  existence; (3) quiddities are known without knowing they exist, and "non-existence" is itself conceived in "a thing is existent or
  non-existent" → non-existent quiddities are conceivable; (4) the builder or tailor conceives the house or shirt first, then brings it to be.
  Then **the generalisation argument**: His essence is what requires knowing; it is not more apt to require knowledge of some knowables than
  others → either it requires none (false) or **all that can be known** (the thesis).
- *Opponents (against prior knowledge)*: (1) **if He knew particulars before they occur, lordship and servanthood would both be void**: what is
  known to occur is necessary, and the necessary is not within anyone's power, so neither God nor the servant has power — yet we find by
  necessity that we can act or refrain at will; (2) the non-existent is sheer nothing and cannot be distinguished; and positing "fixed
  quiddities in non-existence" (the Muʿtazilī *al-maʿdūm shayʾ*) does not help, since even they deny the non-existent essences are composed,
  shaped, coloured or in place — yet you hold He knows they will be so; (3) knowledge is a relation, which presupposes both terms; the
  non-existent object comes to be only by power, which presupposes knowledge → circle.
- *Objections to His knowing ALL knowables*: (1) **infinite regress of reflexive knowings** (knowing that He knows, that He knows that He
  knows, …), each grounded in the previous one → infinitely many causes and effects at once. To "why is knowing-that-one-knows distinct from
  knowing?": the object differs; one can know while heedless of knowing that one knows; if identical, all levels would be actually present
  whenever one knows anything, which is evidently false. To "the known isn't the cause of the knowing": the essence produces each level with
  the prior level as a *condition*, and a cause's condition counts as the cause; besides, each prior level entails the next. (2) The infinite
  cannot be distinguished from other things (what is distinguished is bounded by what lies outside it), but every known is distinguished →
  the infinite is unknowable. (3) Infinitely many known objects → infinitely many attributes in His essence, whether knowledge is relations
  (and they must be real, else He is not a knower) or the philosophers' matching forms; and they are distinct (knowing *this* contradicts not
  knowing *this*, not not-knowing-at-all; one can believe He knows this while heedless that He knows that). But infinite existing attributes
  are impossible: every existing number admits more and less → finite; and each known object itself carries infinite judgments (it differs
  from infinitely many quiddities; an atom can occupy infinitely many places and times, alternately), so the infinite would be multiplied
  infinitely, and multiplying belongs only to the finite.
- ★ **Razi's answers**: (1) the lordship/servanthood objection "points to the claim that [this knowledge] entails *compulsion*" (text prints
  al-ḥayyiz, almost certainly a misprint for **al-jabr**) — to be treated "in the question of jabr"; (2) already refuted at the start of this
  faṣl; (3) the reflexive regress yields causes and effects **without a last term, not without a first** (so it is not the impossible kind);
  (4) each item is distinguished from the others, which suffices to make each knowable; (5) these attachments (taʿalluqāt) are infinitely
  many relations and *relations can be infinite and all obtain*: one is half of two, a third of three, a quarter of four …

## L10132–10291 Bāb 4: God as willing (murīd)
(MSS number it continuously: F17 (S), F7 (M), F8 (T). Plan: meaning of will; proofs; deniers' proofs.)
### F1 The reality of will (L10148–)
- *Issue 1, philosophers*: we find in ourselves that imagining a pure or preponderant benefit produces an inclination (mayl) to get it, and a
  harm an inclination to repel it; these are what we call *irāda* and *karāha*. In this sense will is impossible for God, who is not subject to
  pleasure, pain, benefit or harm. If something else is meant, define it.
  *Mutakallimūn*: **will is an attribute that makes one side of a possible preponderate over the other, without necessitation and without
  bringing into being (takwīn)**. Evidence: (1) the man choosing between two cups or two loaves, or the fugitive from a lion at a fork with two
  equal roads, picks one with no added benefit; (2) the sick man craves fruit but does not will it, and the ascetic wills hard prayers without
  craving them → will ≠ natural inclination.
  *Philosophers' rejoinder*: (1) an influence "not by way of necessity" reopens the problem of the able agent: an agent with every condition
  must act, one lacking any cannot, so "possible-to-influence" is unintelligible; (2) granted, how does will differ from power? "Preponderating"
  is nothing but one side occurring through it, i.e. *takwīn*. Against the examples: the two loaves are never truly equal for the subject (one
  seems nearer or easier; intense hunger fixes the gaze on the first one seen, and getting, keeping and recalling a mental preponderant are
  three different things); and even granting a choice with no preponderant, what drives him is desire for food or flight from the lion, i.e.
  benefit and harm. The sick man weighs present pleasure against future pain; the ascetic weighs present pain against great future benefit.
  As said in the chapter on **"motives and deterrents" (al-dawāʿī wa-l-ṣawārif)**, cash beats credit (al-naqd khayr min al-nasīʾa) as such, but
  a larger credit can outweigh it; if thought and imagination decide, preponderance follows; if not, he wavers. → "we know of will and
  aversion nothing but natural inclination toward benefit and away from harm", impossible in God.
- *Issue 2, the positions* (L10212): **al-Kaʿbī, al-Jāḥiẓ, Abū l-Ḥusayn al-Baṣrī**: His willing an act = His knowing it to be beneficial. For
  oneself that is the motive of need (impossible in God); for others it is the motive of beneficence (dāʿiyat al-iḥsān), which is what applies
  to God. The rest of the mutakallimūn: an attribute beyond that knowledge. Map: *negative* (He acts without being coerced) vs *positive*;
  positive either necessitated by His essence or by an entity (maʿnā) that is either eternal and unchangeable or originated, the originated one
  being in His essence (**Karrāmiyya**) or **in no substrate ("a large group of Muʿtazila")**. In another substrate: "held by no one".
- *Proof for will (the specification argument)*: the world, being originated, began at a definite time though it could have begun earlier or
  later; a specifier is needed. Not power, which relates equally to all times. Not knowledge: knowing the welfare in it would ground God's acts
  in purposes (refuted), and knowing *that* it will occur follows the occurrence, which follows the specification (circle). Life, hearing,
  sight and speech plainly cannot do it → a further attribute; and since it does not act by necessity, it is what was sought. "This is the
  argument in its best form."
- ★ **Razi's objections**: an influence neither necessitating nor bringing-into-being has been shown impossible, so "what we said is a decisive
  proof against what you said". And why not power? Either the will can fit other times too (then it too needs a preponderant, or if not, power
  doesn't need will) or it can attach *only* to that time (then, being eternal and unremovable, God becomes a **necessitating cause by essence
  (mūjib bi-l-dhāt)**, and this issue is a branch of affirming the free agent, and a branch that ruins its root is void; and if the will can be
  fixed to one option, why not power, making will superfluous?).
- *Issue 3, deniers' four arguments*: (1) complete in its causal conditions → the act is necessary; incomplete → impossible; (2) we understand
  will only as inclination to benefit or from harm; (3) it would be eternal or originated, both void; (4) its requiring of preponderance is
  either necessary (→ mūjib bi-l-dhāt) or possible (→ no preponderance amid equality). The bāb closes there, with no further verdict from Razi.
  [Note: Razi's objections in this bāb are entirely on the deniers' side. The bāb ends on aporia, consistent with vol. 3's earlier
  determinism: dāʿī + power → necessary act.]
### F2 Pleasure and pain (L10272–10290)
(MS S: F18.) Bodily pleasure is impossible for God, who is not a body. **Spiritual pleasure: the philosophers unanimously affirm it of the
Necessary.** (a) He loves Himself: knowing a thing's perfection produces love of it, by induction: hearing of the bravery of **Rustam and
Isfandiyār** produces strong love in us, caused only by our belief in their perfections; His knowledge and perfection are supreme → supreme
love. (b) He delights (ibtihāj) in and enjoys His perfections, since knowing one's own perfection produces delight. Corollaries: His delight
relates to ours as His knowledge and perfection relate to ours, i.e. there is **no proportion**; the separate beings (**angels in the
Sharīʿa's tongue, intellects and souls in the philosophers'**) all delight in themselves by degree of perfection, and "the most majestic
self-delighter is the First". [Razi reports this without objecting in this short faṣl.]

## L10294– Bāb 5: God as hearing and seeing (samīʿ baṣīr)
(MS M: F9.) Plan: the quiddity of hearing and seeing; proofs for God; deniers' proofs.
- *Inquiry 1, quiddity (L10304–10354)*: look at Zayd with full knowledge of his form, then shut your eyes: there is a self-evident difference
  though the knowledge persists → **seeing is a state additional to knowledge**. One party: the difference is the form imprinted in the pupil.
  Evidence for imprinting: after-image of the sun's disc after a long look, which one cannot will away; after staring at a green meadow a white
  thing looks greenish-white; the face's image is seen in another's pupil; staring at the sun fatigues and weakens the eye. Another party:
  imprinting happens, but **visual perception is itself distinct**: (1) we see half the sphere of the world, which cannot be imprinted in the
  small eye (the great is not imprinted in the small); (2) being affected is just receiving a matching form, not *unveiling and disclosure*
  (inkishāf, tajallī), which seeing is; (3) if seeing were the impression, a stronger impression would mean better sight, but gazing at the sun
  intensifies the impression while sight weakens. Hearing likewise: we can know what a sound is before hearing it, and hearing is
  self-evidently different. One party reduces the difference to air-waves striking the eardrum (loud sounds can tear it; trumpets feel as if
  they split the brain). Against reducing hearing to that: (1) speech heard *perfectly* through a wall: the wave's shape cannot survive passage
  through dense matter and narrow pores (the open-vs-closed-door point is considered and rejected); (2) we hear a sound *as coming from the
  right or left*, i.e. in its place, while touch, which perceives only what reaches the organ, cannot tell direction; (3) as with sight, more
  impression does not mean more hearing. → ★ **Seeing and hearing are each a state beyond knowledge and beyond the organ's affection, a
  more perfect and stronger unveiling than "knowledge".**
- *Inquiry 2, is there proof of such unveiling for God?* (L10356–): ★ Razi: "though we have shown this state differs from the affection of
  eye and ear, **we suspend judgment (mutawaqqifūn) at another level**": it may be *conditioned* on the organs' affection (impaired organs
  impair the perceptions, sound organs give sound ones); or, though conditioned on organs *for the human soul*, it may occur to the separate
  beings without organs, as existence is conditioned on an agent for possibles yet belongs to the Necessary by Itself without one …
  … (cont. L10360) "these two possibilities stand; on the first, affirming the attribute of God is impossible, on the second not → **suspension
  is obligatory**. **But the premise built on *al-awlā wa-l-akhlaq* (the more fitting and more appropriate) requires affirming it**": He is
  the most perfect existent, so He must have every perfection; seeing and hearing are perfections (whoever knows a thing and then sees it
  feels he has passed from deficiency to perfection, from hiddenness to clarity) → by "the first intellect" (primary reason) we believe He has
  them. [Same method as in vol. 1: demonstrative suspension plus the "more fitting" judgement.] Editor's fn 4 (L10370) refers to *al-Arwāḥ
  al-ʿāliya wa-l-sāfila* (vol. 7).
- *Inquiry 3, the deniers (L10374–10390)*: one cannot see or hear a thing before it exists, so (1) the attribute would change (seeing at
  existence, not before), and change in His attributes is impossible; (2) the perception depends on the perceived existing, so His essence
  would not suffice for the attribute → essence depends on attribute, attribute on another → the Necessary becomes possible. "This is all the
  rational investigation we have summarised in this bāb."
- ★ **Two weak kalām routes, "which we mention and show to be weak"**:
  (1) *Ashʿarī route*: He is living; whatever is living can hear and see; whatever can have a quality has it or its contrary; the contraries of
  hearing and sight (deafness, blindness) are deficiencies, impossible in God. **Six objections**: (i) if "living" means "not impossible that
  He know and have power", why does that entail possible hearing and seeing (the very point at issue)? any other sense needs defining.
  (ii) granting life: His life differs from ours in quiddity, and things differing in quiddity need not share concomitants (our life makes
  appetite, pain, pleasure, ignorance and opinion possible, His does not); even with like lives, the *recipient* must be receptive, and His
  particular essence may not be (existence "validates all attributes" yet most attributes are impossible for Him); or a condition may be
  missing. (iii) if deafness and blindness mean mere *absence* of hearing and sight, "their absence is a deficiency" restates the claim; if
  they are positive contraries, prove it. (iv) why must a substrate receptive of two contraries have one of them? Air is receptive of all
  tastes and colours yet has none; we know the doings of the market crowd without willing or disliking them. (v) what is "deficiency"? if it
  means what prevents Him being creator, show that lacking these does that. (vi) why is deficiency impossible for Him? if by self-evidence,
  then a shorter route is available ("the hearing-seeing living one is perfect, the other deficient, and self-evidence judges God must be
  described with perfection"); if by consensus (ijmāʿ), weak: **"the verses proving consensus is a proof are far weaker in indication than the
  verses proving He is hearing and seeing"**, so if a scriptural premise is allowed, cite those verses directly.
  (2) *Muʿtazilī route*: He is living with no defect (āfa); every such living being perceives perceptibles when present (sound eye + present
  visible → sight; burning coal on a sound body → heat felt). Objections: (i) His being-living differs from ours, and His essence differs in
  quiddity; (ii) life may require perception only given conditions absent in Him. A later Muʿtazilī reply: conditions impossible for God cease
  to be conditions. ★ Razi: false; a missing condition removes the conditioned; **if that reasoning held, reductio (qiyās al-khulf) would
  collapse** ("the concomitant is absent, so it is no longer a concomitant, so the antecedent can exist without it"). (iii) life may produce
  *sensory* perception generally, but seeing and hearing do not arise from life alone. "God knows best."

## L10490–10630 Bāb 6: God as speaking (mutakallim)
(Orig. "F10".)
- *Issue 1, reality of speech*: we know by necessity we have command, prohibition, statement and question. These are not the mere words:
  (1) the formulae differ across languages while command is one; (2) command's quiddity is unchangeable while conventions change; (3) a
  form for "statement" could have been assigned to "request", but a statement's quiddity cannot turn into a request. **On "request" (ṭalab)**:
  either = willing the act from another (prohibition = willing the omission), or something other than will, since one may will an act and
  not command it, or command without willing (when the command has another purpose). The first party's replies: commanding = acting to make
  known that one wills; a command one does not will is "not really a command, just a word suggesting will". **Language's origin**: to make
  another know what is in our hearts we must do some act; people agreed on assigning words to meanings because (1) we must breathe out warmed
  air anyway, the voice cut at different places of articulation (makhārij) yields letters whose combinations can map infinitely many meanings,
  unlike other acts; (2) sounds exist when needed and vanish when not; (3) producing them is quasi-natural. → **"the speaker (mutakallim) is
  the one who *makes* speech (fāʿil al-kalām)"**: speech is a conventional act indicating an inclination or aversion in the heart. "Rational;
  no room for dispute."
- *Issue 2, proving He speaks*: it follows from His being a free agent who knows particulars. Those who reduce ṭalab to will: He wills some
  things and dislikes others, and **creating sounds in a particular body** that by convention indicate His will and dislike "is speech". Those
  who make ṭalab a distinct meaning need three things: the intelligible difference from will; proof He has it ("they have no good proof, as
  will be explained"); and ★ **their claim that it is eternal, "which is far-fetched" (baʿīd)**. **Six rational arguments against an eternal
  command or statement**: (1) command = imposing an obligation (ilzām), and it is necessarily known that one cannot say "O Zayd, I oblige
  you" when Zayd does not exist ("if this is not necessary knowledge, the intellect has none"); (2) "We sent Noah" (Q71:1), "Adam disobeyed"
  (Q20:121): what is reported precedes the report, so an eternal report would be preceded by something. To the objection from knowledge:
  ★ **"we have mentioned that the correct doctrine (al-madhhab al-ṣaḥīḥ) here is Abū l-Ḥusayn al-Baṣrī's: knowledge changes when the known
  changes"** [an explicit verdict; compare F6's leaning]; (3) once Zayd prays the dawn prayer, either the obligation persists (he can never
  discharge it, even after a million performances) or it ceases (the eternal cannot cease); (4) abrogation (naskh), lifting or ending a ruling
  (editor: the Qurʾān abrogating the Torah), means it ceased after existing, and what ceases is not eternal; (5) an eternal attribute's
  attachments are essential and necessary, so it would attach to everything it can; since they deny rational good and evil, everything can
  be commanded and forbidden → all things commanded and forbidden at once; (6) as knowledge changes with its objects, so a report with what it
  reports, and what changes is not eternal. "Evident rational arguments that this ṭalab and khabar cannot be eternal, whether ṭalab is will or
  not."
- *Arguments for eternal speech*: (1) the Ashʿarī "living → can speak → speech or its contrary (muteness, silence), a deficiency"; (2) we agree
  He speaks: not by His essence (agreed), so by speech; an originated speech would be in His essence (events in God), in another (then He
  could be ignorant by another's ignorance), or in no substrate (agreed impossible; and a thing's attribute is in it) → eternal; (3) speech is
  a perfection (then always had) or a deficiency (then never); (4) a speaker is more perfect than a non-speaker; we speak; if God did not in
  eternity, we would at our existence be more perfect than He was in eternity. ★ **Replies**: (1) see hearing and seeing; and **silence is
  no deficiency; the deficiency is saying "O Zayd, pray! O ʿAmr, fast!" when they do not exist; a man alone in his house calling "rider,
  mount! standing one, come forward!" would be judged mad**; (2) if "speaking" means He did acts indicating His will and dislike, granted, but
  that implies no attribute called speech; anything more is denied, and **"the agreement is only verbal"**; (3)–(4) commanding and forbidding
  with no one commanded is itself the deficiency and folly (safah). "Scriptural and verbal arguments contain many sayings not fitting for
  this book."
  [Key result: in *al-Maṭālib* Razi rejects the eternity of the Ashʿarī *kalām nafsī* on rational grounds, at least for command and report,
  and defines divine speech as created sounds that indicate His will. This departs strongly from standard Ashʿarism.]

## L10640–10680 Bāb 7: God as eternal and enduring (qadīm bāqī)
(Orig. "F11".) Two views: eternity and endurance are His very essence, or two attributes subsisting in it. *For attributes*: (1) "eternal" (no
beginning) and "enduring" (stable existence) are understood by us, while His particular essence is not understood (shown earlier) → they
differ; (2) "the eternal enduring" is divided into body, substance, accident or something else, and what is divided is common to the parts,
so it differs from each; (3) "God's essence is eternal and enduring" is informative, "God's essence is an essence" is not. *Against*: (1) the
essence would need the attribute → necessary through another. Reply: the essence, as that very essence, may *necessitate* its own
permanence; the absurdity follows only if something beyond the essence causes its permanence, and on this account the permanence is still an
attribute. (2) permanence would need its own permanence → regress; and if self-permanent it is more fit to be the self-standing essence; if
permanent through the essence → circle, through another → regress. No verdict given ("the rational summary of this bāb").

## L10686–10704 Bāb 8: God as living (ḥayy)
(Orig. "F12".) "Living" means "it is valid that He know and have power"; since He is knowing and able, and what exists is not impossible,
this holds. *Objection*: impossibility is a privative notion, so its negation is existent; "living" is a positive attribute and description
of
the essence, so not the essence itself → an existent attribute. *Another view*: life is an existent attribute that makes knowing and power
possible; otherwise this validity would belong to this essence no more than to any other. *Reply*: that holds only if essences were equal in
quiddity; His differs. And essences also differ in the validity of being alive, so if difference needs an attribute, life needs another →
regress; if the particular essence explains one difference, why not the other?

## L10710– Bāb 9 "Kalimāt fī l-ṣifāt" (editor's title)
Editor's fn (L10724, not Razi): the MSS present the remaining faṣls of book 3 as "explanatory faṣls" numbered 13–22; the editor groups them
under this bāb title.
### F1 How many attributes? (L10714–10722)
The mutakallimūn restrict them to eight: living, knowing, able, willing, hearing, seeing, speaking, enduring. Asked "any more?", they say no:
there is no proof of more, and what has no proof must be denied; or, no number would be more apt than another, so infinitely many unknown
attributes. ★ **Razi**: "you know from logic that **not knowing a thing does not yield knowing its non-existence**"; "no number is more apt",
if meant in the mind, yields only suspension; if meant in reality, it cannot be proved. **What reason proves we affirm; where reason proves
neither existence nor non-existence, suspension is obligatory.**
### F2 Does He know by His essence or by an entity (maʿnā)? (L10728–)
(Orig. F14.) "The most important thing is to fix the locus of disagreement." Power is the validity of acting, which is not the essence itself
(one can know it without knowing the essence); knowing is awareness and disclosure, which comes about only by a **specific relation (nisba)**
between knower and known. These relations ("attachment", taʿalluq) must be affirmed or His knowing and power denied. Positions: some
mutakallimūn hold three items (essence, the attribute "knowledge", its attachment); others four or five (essence, knowledge, *ʿālimiyya* (the
state of being a knower), and attachment for one or both; "I do not know how exactly their views go"). ★ **"As for us, we affirm only two
things: the essence and the relation called *ʿālimiyya*, and we hold that this relation is not the essence itself but something additional
to it, existing in it. This is the purely rational question."** Merely *verbal* questions: are the attributes "other than" the essence or
"neither it nor other"; are they called "eternals" (qudamāʾ); "accidents"; "subsisting in / inhering in / existing in" or only "existing by"
the essence. "The well-known report is that the philosophers and Muʿtazila deny the attributes and the Ṣifātiyya affirm them." [Editor's
long fn (L10760–10788, not Razi): the Muʿtazila do not deny the attributes; he cites ʿAbd al-Jabbār's *Sharḥ al-Uṣūl al-Khamsa*, *al-Mughnī*,
*al-Muḥīṭ bi-l-Taklīf*, *Tanzīh al-Qurʾān ʿan al-Maṭāʿin*, *Tathbīt Dalāʾil al-Nubuwwa*, and al-Zamakhsharī; they deny only bodily members.]
Two stations: (1) knowledge and power, as these relations, are additional to the essence; (2) are they necessary in themselves, necessitated
by the essence, or by an entity that necessitates the relations?
- *Station 1, eight arguments that knowledge and power are not the essence itself*: (1) "knowing" contradicts "not knowing" but not "not
  existent", and vice versa; (2) after proving a necessary eternal cause, reason still suspends on whether it knows and has power and needs a
  separate proof; (3) "God's existent essence is existent" is uninformative, "… is knowing and able" informative; (4) the relation between knower
  and known is an attribute of the essence and depends on it; relations are said relative to another, self-standing essences are not; (5) able
  ≠ knowing (each can be without the other) → one thing would be two; (6) if knowing = power, whatever He knows He could do, so the necessary
  and the impossible would be objects of power; (7) once a power's object exists it is no longer an object of power (making the existent is
  impossible) yet it remains known; (8) knowledge and power have different definitions, rules and properties, so they are not synonyms; the
  three notions (knowing, able, existent essence) are not three self-standing entities (agreed and evident), so one is the subject and the
  others attributes.
- *Deniers*. **Philosophers**: (1) the attributes are necessary by themselves (but there is only one Necessary, and they subsist in another so
  depend on it) or possible (then caused by the essence, which would be both receiver and agent, impossible; and "need arises only at the
  time of origination, else making-to-be of what already is", so caused attributes would be originated); (2) the God of the world is simple
  or composite; a composite needs its parts, which are other than it → possible; subject plus attribute is composite → He is an absolute
  unity (fard muṭlaq). **Muʿtazila, general arguments**: (1) His being-a-knower is necessary (else a maker would make Him a knower, and God
  would be a servant), and the necessary has no cause; (2) **many eternals (qudamāʾ)**: eternity is positive and shared, so they differ by
  constituent parts, which are themselves eternal and so composite without end, or they are alike in their whole quiddity (essence becomes
  attribute and vice versa → many gods); (3) a multiplicity of eternals is agreed false; (4) inherence means the attribute is in a place
  following its substrate; without this neither would be more apt to subsist in the other → spatial God; (5) His divinity is complete without
  the attributes (they are superfluous) or not (He needs another); (6) essence alone sufficient → attributes idle; attributes sufficient →
  essence idle; neither → whatever arises at their union regresses or adds nothing → divinity is a concomitant of the essence as such.
  **Against knowledge specifically**: (1) a knowledge would attach to what ours attaches to, in the same respect, so would be like ours →
  both eternal or both originated …
  … (cont. L10876) (2) He would know by one knowledge (impossible: one can know He knows *this* while heedless that He knows *that*), by
  finitely many (then one knowledge would attach to many objects, since His knowables are infinite), or by infinitely many (every existing
  number is finite); (3) a knowledge would be known either by itself (a relation of a thing to itself is impossible) or by another → circle or
  regress.
- ★ **Razi's answers**: to the philosophers: (1) rests on "one thing cannot be both receiver and agent", already refuted; (2) why not say the
  essence is necessary in itself and *necessitates* the ʿālimiyya and qādiriyya? To the Muʿtazila (editor's gloss on "if you mean": "O Qāḍī",
  i.e. ʿAbd al-Jabbār): (1) if "necessary" means necessary by the essence itself, that is the very point at issue; if necessary absolutely, a
  chain essence → maʿnā → ʿālimiyya keeps it necessary; (2) they themselves admit ʿālimiyya and qādiriyya (aḥwāl), so whatever they say
  against the maʿnā we turn on those; (3) "distinct eternals": if "distinct" means different realities, granted, and that is the question
  itself; if it means separable in place or time, who says every two existents must be? (4) the spatial account of inherence was refuted in
  the chapter on substance and accident, and "this is no more apt than that" is "a feeble, weak premise"; (5)–(6) why not: essence →
  maʿnā → ʿālimiyya? Against knowledge specifically: sharing an effect (attachment to the same object) does not entail sharing the
  quiddity of the cause; arguments 2–3 apply equally to *attachments*. "All these proofs are weak", and **they only touch those who explain
  the attachments by attributes or entities subsisting in the essence; "we do not say that: we have shown the attachments are additional to
  the essence, and for us they are explained by the essence itself (muʿallala bi-nafs al-dhāt)"**, so none of them touches us.
- *Station 2* (L10912): qādiriyya (validity of making and leaving) and ʿālimiyya (a specific relation) are not self-standing, hence possible
  in themselves, hence caused, and the cause is the essence (anything else would make the Necessary depend on another for its concomitants).
  Concomitants are immediate or mediated; the essence may produce them directly or through one or several intermediaries; both possible. ★
  **"But since the essence must be admitted to necessitate these relations, directly or not, and the intermediary is unknown, by *al-awlā
  wa-l-akhlaq* we must delete the intermediary."** Parallel: proving a free agent from the world's origination, asked "why can't the free agent
  be the effect of a necessitating cause?", we say: since we must admit a necessary existent and a free agent, it is more fitting to identify
  them and delete intermediaries. The same principle applies to the attributes in His essence. [This is Razi's Ockham-like principle.]
- *Proponents of maʿānī (entities)*: (1) knowledge "here" (shāhid) is an attribute that attaches, so "there" (ghāʾib) too, since quiddities do
  not differ between seen and unseen; (2) in us ʿālimiyya arises after not being, so knowledge is an existing attribute, and the bare body
  cannot attach to the known; (3) knowledge is what attaches to the known, so if the essence attached, God's essence would *be* knowledge.
  Replies: (1) knowledge here too is nothing but this attachment, this awareness; (2) when this relation arises in a body, knowledge and
  awareness have arisen; a further attribute bearing it is denied; (3) knowledge is not "a thing attached" but the attachment itself, so the
  essence's having the attachment does not make it "knowledge".

### L10938–10948 Bāb 9 F3 Enumerating God's attributes
(Orig. F15.) The philosophers agree that His attributes are *negations* (not a body, not a substance, not spatial), *relations* (generous,
bringer-to-be, bestower) or compounds of the two. ★ Razi: they themselves undo this, since for them knowledge is a matching *form*, which
is neither negation nor relation, so they admit a real attribute (ṣifa ḥaqīqiyya) in His essence. "We showed that this restriction can be
established only by way of *al-awlā wa-l-akhlaq*"; and if knowledge is a relation, affirming it does not breach the restriction.

### L10952–10974 Bāb 9 F4 Is bringing-into-being (takwīn) the thing brought into being, or other?
(Orig. F16. The Māturīdī question of *takwīn*, though Razi does not name the Māturīdīs.) Analogues "here": is moving the motion, blackening the
blackness? "Obscure, with great depth." *For difference*: we explain "why did it come to be?" by "because the able one made it", so if making
= its existence, it would exist by itself and the agent would have no effect; and takwīn is not power (He can create many suns and moons
without creating them) → a third attribute. *Against*: an eternal takwīn implies an eternal product (takwīn holds only with the coming-to-be;
while the act stays in non-existence, the agent has done nothing to it) → eternity of the world; an originated takwīn needs another takwīn →
regress. No verdict here: "we will return to this in the book on origination and eternity" (i.e. vol. 4).

### L10982– Bāb 9 F5 Dividing God's names (taqsīm asmāʾ Allāh)
(Orig. F17.) [Razi's *Lawāmiʿ al-Bayyināt*-style material.]
- **Division 1: a name signifies the essence, a part of it, or something outside it.**
  (a) *Names of the essence itself*: objection: His particular essence is unknown to creatures, and naming requires the meaning to be known to
  speaker and hearer → impossible; "the ancient philosophers said: a reality created intellects cannot grasp has no name, except to explain it
  as *necessary of existence by itself*". ★ **Reply**: what is established is that *we* do not know it, not that knowing it is rationally
  impossible; even if it is, God may honour some servants with that knowledge; or the angelic holy spirits may know it; or the reality may have
  a specific name which **God teaches to some servants, whose mention makes the pure holy spirits obey, or gives its rememberer a power of
  spirit attainable no other way** — "all these are possible". [The Greatest Name; compare *al-Sirr al-Maktūm*.]
  (b) *Names of a part*: impossible, since He is simple.
  (c) *Names of something outside*: a real attribute, a relation, a negation, or their combinations (seven sorts).
- **Real attributes**: existence, modes of existence, or a third.
  *Existence*: "mawjūd" can mean "found" (as in Q4:64 "they would have found (la-wajadū) God relenting") or "really there", so it is not
  univocal; hence **"some monotheists expressed His existence by the Persian *hastī* (al-hasqiyya, sic)"**, which is unambiguous. Names
  signifying the essence-as-existent:
  (1) **al-Ḥaqq**: ḥaqq = existent, bāṭil = non-existent; a belief or avowal of what is necessary "deserves" affirmation, of what is
  impossible deserves removal. The necessary in itself is pure ḥaqq; the impossible is pure bāṭil; **the possible, taken in itself, is
  non-existent, i.e. bāṭil and hālik, "everything perishes save His face" (Q28:88), and perishes *forever* since possibility never leaves it.
  "Hence they said: there is no existent in reality but God."** He "verifies the truth by His words" (Q10:82): He makes other things ḥaqq.
  His knowledge and avowal are the truest of truths, since their object cannot change.
  (2) **Shayʾ ("thing")**: **Jahm b. Ṣafwān** forbade it, not denying His existence, only the name. *For*: "Say: what thing is greatest in
  testimony? Say: God" (Q6:19); "everything perishes except His face" (Q28:88; face = essence, and the excepted belongs to the class); in
  language, "thing" is either synonymous with "existent" (for those who deny that the non-existent is a thing) or more general (for those who
  affirm it), so it applies either way. *Against*: "Creator of every thing" (Q6:102), which would make Him create Himself, and specification
  is unacceptable here (you may except a small part from "all", never the greatest); "there is nothing like His like (laysa ka-mithlihi shayʾ)"
  (Q42:11) says the like of His like is not a thing, and He is the like of His like, and taking the *kāf* as redundant is far-fetched since
  this lofty book should be read as meaningful; "to God belong the most beautiful names" (Q7:180), and "thing" names what is common to vile
  and noble alike, so it is not beautiful. "**A purely verbal question.**"
  (3) **Māhiyya**: from "mā hiya?". Pharaoh's "And what is the Lord of the worlds?" (Q26:23) went unrebuked by Moses. ★ **"A lofty and noble
  inquiry"**: Pharaoh debated Moses twice (editor: one session repeated). First he asked **"who (man) is your Lord?"** (Q20:49), which asks
  for an attribute, and Moses answered "who gave each thing its creation then guided" (Q20:50): **creation = governing bodies, guidance =
  governing spirits**, which covers everything self-standing besides God (spatial or not), so Pharaoh was silenced. Later he asked with
  **"what (mā)"**, which asks for the quiddity; Moses answered with attributes ("Lord of the heavens and earth"); Pharaoh: "do you not hear?"
  (the answer doesn't fit the question); Moses again with an attribute; Pharaoh: "your messenger is mad"; Moses: "Lord of east and west … if
  you would understand" (Q26:28), i.e. if you were rational you would know there is no other answer. Reason: a thing is defined by itself
  (circular), by its parts (He has none), or by its concomitants and effects, which must be manifest, and **the most manifest is the creation
  of this world → God's essence can be made known only through His creatures**.
  (4) **Ḥaqīqa**: faʿīla is active or passive; in God's case active, **He who makes realities real (yuḥaqqiq al-ḥaqāʾiq)**; in possibles'
  case passive.
  (5) **Dhāt**: a word for possession ("dhāt māl"); essences as such are unknown and known only by attributes, so "dhāt" names what is fit to
  bear attributes; God's is the most deserving, being most self-standing and needing no substrate or recipient.
  (6) **Nafs**: "You know what is in my soul and I know not what is in Yours" (Q5:116); for Him nafs means essence, not body.
- **Names of modes of existence**, *necessity*: (1) **wājib al-wujūd li-dhātihi**, close to the **Persian *Khudāy* < *khud-āy*, "came by
  itself"**, i.e. existed by itself, exactly matching wājib al-wujūd; (2) **al-Qawī** (Q51:58): what cannot be changed; or ("better") *qawī* =
  affecting others, *matīn* = unaffected by others; (3) **al-Qayyūm**: self-subsistent (existing by and for Himself, needing nothing) and
  making others subsist (bringing all else to be) → perfect in subsisting.
  *Permanence*: (1) **al-Qadīm**: without beginning (also used for "long-lasting": Q12:95, Q36:39). Those who make eternity a *negative*
  attribute are wrong, since it is the negation of prior non-existence and negation of negation is affirmation; those who say He is eternal by
  an eternity subsisting in Him are wrong too (regress); (2) **al-Azalī**; (3) **al-Abadī**: no end; (4) **al-Bāqī** (Q20:73): definitions
  listed, including "the Real endures by His endurance, creation by His making-endure". Past permanence = qidam and azal; future = abad and
  sarmad (from *sard*, continuity, with an intensifying *mīm*). (5) **al-Wārith** (Q19:40, Q57:10): He lent possessions to servants, who die,
  and they return to the true Owner; "Whose is the kingdom today? God's, the One, the Subduer" (Q40:16). (6) **al-Awwal, al-Ākhir, al-Ẓāhir,
  al-Bāṭin**: descending from cause to effect He is First; ascending from effect to cause He is Last; **Manifest because effects show the
  cause's existence; Hidden because knowing the effect gives no knowledge of the cause's quiddity.** "Secrets of these four": the First is the
  unique prior one, proved by the contingency argument (not all existents possible, since the whole depends on possibles; not all necessary,
  since two necessaries are impossible; so one Necessary, principle of all, prior by essence and causality). **The mutakallimūn add**: need
  cannot be in the state of endurance (that would be making the existent), so all else is originated, and **He is First also in time**
  (existing when all else did not).
- **Names of transcendence (tanzīh)**: (1) **al-Quddūs** (Q59:23, Q62:1): *quds* = purity (Bayt al-Maqdis; the Garden as *ḥaẓīrat al-quds*;
  Gabriel as *rūḥ al-quds*, pure of betrayal in transmitting revelation). (2) **al-Salām**: Quddūs = free of deficiency in essence and
  attributes, Salām = in acts. (3) **al-ʿAzīz**: three conditions (rare, greatly needed, hard to reach); the sun is unique and greatly
  needed but easy to reach, so not ʿazīz; perfection in each (unique and impossible to duplicate, not just actually unique like the sun; the
  source of all benefits; impossible to comprehend and known only by His guidance, His blessings beyond all thanks) belongs to God alone →
  **the absolute ʿAzīz**. (4) **al-Jabbār**: (i) lofty and unreachable (a tall palm is *jabbāra*), beyond thoughts, sight and the knowledge of
  sages; (ii) the mender (jabartu l-kasr), who sets right creatures' bodies and souls; (iii) **from ijbār, compelling: "He compelled creatures
  to what He willed, whether they willed it or not; nothing runs in His dominion but what He wills."** (5) **al-Mutakabbir**: who sees all as
  small beside Himself; true seeing makes pride rightful and false seeing makes it blameworthy; ḥadīth qudsī "Grandeur (kibriyāʾ) is My cloak,
  majesty (ʿaẓama) My waist-wrap" (editor: a solitary ḥadīth, āḥād). (6)–(7) **al-ʿAliyy, al-Kabīr**: height is sensible (the Throne above
  the Footstool, only for bodies, so not His) or intelligible (Q58:11, degrees). Every scale of perfection (cause/effect, necessary/possible,
  absolutely perfect/not; knowledge, power, life, permanence, existence, mercy) has God at its top → **His "aboveness" (fawqiyya, Q6:18,
  Q16:50) is rank, not place or direction**. It reduces to a negative name (nothing equals Him in nobility) or a relational one (He has power
  over all). Kabīr / Mutakabbir / Akbar / Kibriyāʾ: Kabīr is not by bulk but by rank ("great in religion"), as greatest of beings or as too
  great to resemble creatures (names of tanzīh). **"Allāhu akbar" opens the prayer so that the worshipper, knowing God is greater than all
  else, attaches his mind and heart to nothing but God.** Kibriyāʾ as the "cloak" shows it is higher and further from creatures' imaginings
  than ʿaẓama (the waist-wrap).
  (9)–(10) **al-Wāḥid, al-Aḥad** (Q2:163, Q112:1): oneness means no multiplicity in the essence, or no contrary or rival. Definitions of the
  first: (i) a thing that is not divided ("thing" excludes the non-existent; one man or one garment can be divided); (ii) what admits no
  adding or removing (a man without a hand); (iii) what is not a number, a number being half the sum of its two flanks (2 = (1+3)/2), while
  one has only one flank. On (iii) the atom is a true unit; objection: then "one" suggests the least of the least, a deficiency. Reply: the
  atom is called small because it can be touched and joined by others …
  … (cont. L11396) the atom's smallness comes from its being able to be joined by others (alone it is "small", joined "large"); God cannot be
  joined, so this "one" implies no smallness.
  - *Deniers of attributes on oneness*: whoever affirms many attributes subsisting in the essence cannot hold divine unity, since essence plus
    attributes are many and admit "adding and removing" ("able but not knowing", until disproved). Some said: **whoever affirms eight
    attributes makes God "the ninth of nine"; Q5:73 declares unbelief on whoever says "the third of three", so a ninth of nine is "three times
    three", deeper in unbelief.** [Editor's fn: the verse condemns the Catholic Christians' three gods.] *Oneness in sense 2* (no equal):
    nothing equals Him in necessity of existence, knowledge of infinitely many knowables, or power over all possibles; the deniers say "no
    equal in eternity", which those affirming eternal attributes contradict.
  - *Corollaries*: (1) **one in essence** (no quantitative-sensible composition like a body's, no intellectual one like genus plus
    difference), **in attributes** (nothing equals Him in existence by essence, all-knowledge, all-power, independence), **in acts** (nothing
    else is the source of all possibles, directly or through intermediaries); (2) *aḥad* vs *wāḥid*: wāḥid begins counting (one, two, three),
    aḥad does not; in negation aḥad is more general ("mā fī l-dār aḥad" is total negation); in affirmation **aḥad is said only of God (He has
    reserved it), as al-Raḥmān (exclusive) vs al-Raḥīm (shared)**, hence no article in "huwa Allāhu aḥad", since it is already definite;
    (3) ★ **the three names of Q112:1 as three stations of the travellers to God**: (i) *Huwa* for **the muqarrabūn** (highest), who look at
    quiddities as such, see that the possible taken without its cause is pure nothing, and so "see no existent but God"; an absolute pointer
    is determinate only if there is one thing, so "He" suffices and needs no distinguishing attribute; (ii) *Huwa Allāh* for **aṣḥāb
    al-yamīn**, who see other existents alongside the Real and need an attribute to distinguish Him; (iii) *Allāhu aḥad* for **aṣḥāb al-shimāl**
    (weakest), who allow more than one god, to annul shirk. Further: creatures point first to relational attributes and second to negative
    ones; "Allāh" (He who deserves worship, i.e. independent in making and originating, i.e. perfect power and wisdom) gathers all the
    relational attributes, and "Aḥad" gathers all the negative ones (no sensible or intellectual composition, hence not spatial, not in a
    substrate or substrate of anything, no like, contrary or rival; two necessaries would be composite). Objection: a reality qualified by
    aḥadiyya makes reality plus aḥadiyya plus their sum, "a third of three". Answer: aḥadiyya is a *concomitant* of the reality "which can be
    expressed only by *Huwa*", hence **"the most majestic name for the verifiers (muḥaqqiqūn) is *Huwa Allāh*"**. "Huwa Allāh" shows all depend
    on Him and "Aḥad" that He needs none: this is the true master (sayyid), hence **al-Ṣamad**. Ṣamad as "solid, without cavity" is
    impossible for God, so it means freedom from all composition, i.e. the *proof* of aḥad. Three branches: **"He begets not"** (a child is a
    separated part producing a like whole, impossible for the partless; **another reading: no necessitating-by-essence (ījāb) or generation
    (tawallud) in His acting on creatures**); **"nor is begotten"** (the sayyid needs none); **"none is equal to Him"** (a second necessary
    would make both composite). (4) *Waḥīd* (Q74:11 "Leave Me with the one I created alone"): if "alone" describes the Creator, Waḥīd is a
    name: alone in eternity; independent in governing mulk and malakūt, without matter, duration, instrument or equipment (mādda, mudda, āla,
    ʿudda); unique in majestic attributes. (5) *Tawḥīd* = judging or knowing a thing to be one. ★ **"The station of tawḥīd is too narrow for
    speech: when you predicate anything of the Real there is a subject, a predicate and their sum, which make three; the intellect knows it,
    speech cannot reach it."**
- (11) **al-Ṣamad**: from *ṣamada* (to resort to in needs), or without cavity, meaning free of composition. (12) **al-Badīʿ** (Q2:117):
  matchless, or *mubdiʿ* (originator), whose acts come at once without prior matter, duration, instrument or equipment. (13) **al-Ghaniyy**
  (Q10:68; Q47:38): the necessary by itself cannot become necessary by another (if the other is removed and it remains, it was not necessary by
  it; if it does not remain, it was not necessary by itself), so it has no attachment to another; everything else needs Him in quiddity,
  existence and attributes. (14) **al-Qayyūm** (Q2:255, Q20:111; **ʿUmar's reading "al-Ḥayy al-Qayyām"**): self-subsisting (Q3:18 "maintaining
  justice") and making others subsist. *Concomitants*: unicity; not spatial (every spatial thing is divisible quantitatively, and also
  intellectually, by its general spatiality plus its specificity), so not in a direction; not in a substrate (neither an accident in a subject
  nor a form in matter); (some say) knowledge = presence of the known's reality to the knower, so the self-subsistent knows itself and,
  being cause of all, all things; (for originationists) all else is originated; (6) ★ **all possibles rest on Him, mediately or immediately,
  "so the doctrine of *qaḍāʾ and qadar* follows necessarily"**. → "*al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm* encompasses all the high inquiries of theology."
  Qayyūm is negative (independence) and relational (making others subsist). **Ibn ʿAbbās: "the greatest name of God is al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm"**;
  whoever knows this cuts his heart off from creatures. **Abū Yazīd al-Bisṭāmī**: "Enough trust (tawakkul) for you: see no helper but Him, no
  cultivator of your provision but Him, no witness of your deeds but Him."
- (15–16) **al-Jalīl / Dhū l-Jalāl** (Q55:27, 78; "jalīl" itself is not Qurʾānic): **al-Kabīr = perfection of essence, al-Jalīl =
  perfection of attributes (negative and positive), al-ʿAẓīm = perfection of essence, attributes and acts.** Faʿīl read as masdar (He honours
  believers), passive (deserves honour) or active (has majesty); two Sufi sayings. (17) **al-ʿAẓīm**: not bulk; great in essence (intellect
  cannot encompass it, as sight cannot a huge body), in attributes (no proportion), in acts (owner of dominion): negative, relational and
  active respectively. (18–19) **al-Mājid, al-Majīd** (Q85:15): perfect nobility (Q50:1), or amplitude and generosity. (20) **al-Subbūḥ**:
  not Qurʾānic but agreed on by the umma; morphology (faʿʿūl normally has fatḥa except subbūḥ, quddūs, dhurrūḥ); tasbīḥ = "removing God from all
  evil" (a ḥadīth) or from *sabaḥa*, going about (Q73:7). Angels' tasbīḥ (Q21:20, 2:30, 37:165–6); a report on the three heavens' angels
  (standing, bowing, prostrating), which shows **degrees of the angels in gnosis, love and servanthood**. "Everything glorifies Him with His
  praise but you do not understand their glorification" (Q17:44): one party says it is *verbal* tasbīḥ, since the indication of artefacts to
  the Maker would be understood (Q31:25); the verifiers answer that **creatures' indication of His transcendence is one of the subtlest
  branches of gnosis**, so not understood by all; and **the indication of being (dalāla dhātiyya) beats that of speech**: speech can lie, is
  conventional and changeable, reaches only the outer ear (the essential one reaches the intellect, which is nobler), and vanishes (the
  rational indication endures). (21) **al-Ṭāhir**: pure of defects (Q33:33, the Prophet's family; Q9:28, "the polytheists are najas").
  (22) **al-Ṭayyib** (ḥadīth "God is good and accepts only the good"): pure *and* beneficial. (23–24) **al-Fard, al-Witr** (ḥadīth "God is witr
  and loves witr"): seven reasons odd is nobler than even (oneness is His attribute and pairing creatures'; every pair needs the one; an odd
  number contains both an odd and an even part; indivisibility is strength; all numbers arise from one; odd plus even is odd, so odd
  "prevails"; unity attaches to every numerical rank, "one ten, one hundred").
- **Names of power**: **al-Qādir** (Q6:65, 77:23); **al-Qadīr** (not among the 99 but frequent; Q67:1). Morphology: *faʿʿāl* for frequency
  (craft names: ḥaddād, bannāʾ, najjār, fallāḥ, khayyāṭ); *faʿīl* or *faʿūl* when the quality is like a fixed disposition (qadīr, khabīr,
  naṣīr; ṣabūr, shakūr, ghafūr), hence qadīr and ʿalīm outnumber qādir and ʿālim in the Qurʾān. **al-Muqtadir** (Q54:55): iftaʿala signals
  complete power (acting as He wishes, unhindered); compare *kasaba / iktasaba* (Q2:286): **good is "kasb" and evil "iktisāb" because one does
  good for self and others with less zeal, but evil only for oneself, for pleasure, with more effort, and against constant legal
  deterrents**. **al-Qawiyy al-Matīn**: active power vs strong disposition not to be affected (matn = back, the support of animals), i.e.
  He cannot be affected, being necessary in essence and all attributes.
- **Names of knowledge**: ʿilm (Q31:34, 2:255, 4:166, 35:11, 11:14), ʿĀlim (Q59:22), ʿAllām (Q5:116), Aʿlam (Q17:54); *muʿallim* (Q55:2, 4:113)
  is not used of God "because it suggests a kind of lowliness"; ʿAlīm (Q67:13); *ʿallāma* not allowed, since it is said of one who passed from
  little to much knowledge by effort and exercise. (2) **al-Laṭīf** (Q67:14): (i) so small as to be imperceptible, impossible for Him, so
  read as its concomitant: the senses cannot reach Him (tanzīh); (ii) knowing subtleties ("laṭīf al-yad", a skilled craftsman); (iii) kind
  to servants in ways they do not know (Q42:19); (iv) **al-Ghazālī** (in *al-Maqṣad al-Asnā*): whoever knows the subtleties of welfare and
  delivers it gently, without harshness. Not the wisdom in heavens, stars, four elements and three kingdoms, only **the morsel**: countless
  people (preparers of the soil, sowers, irrigators, reapers, cleaners, millers, bakers) cooperate in a mouthful, "whose explanation would not
  fit in volumes". He is Ḥakīm in governing, Jawād in bringing to be, Muṣawwir in ordering, ʿAdl in placing everything right, Laṭīf in
  leaving no subtle kindness undone; "no one knows these names who does not know these acts." (3) **al-Khabīr**: knows the inner reality (for
  creatures, knowledge reached by testing), or = *mukhbir* (informer). (4) **al-Shahīd** (Q41:53, 3:18): ʿAlīm absolutely, Khabīr of hidden
  things, Shahīd of manifest ones; present (Q2:185) by knowledge or sight; the witness who settles disputes, i.e. who made His unicity, justice
  and majesty clear by proofs; or the one witnessed to by servants. (5) **al-Ḥafīẓ** (Q2:255, 12:64, 15:9): opposite of forgetting, i.e.
  unalterable knowledge, or opposite of neglect, guarding heavens and earth and the divine books from distortion (editor: the Jews distorted
  the Torah, the Christians the Gospel). (6) **al-Raqīb** (Q5:117, 50:18): al-Khaṭṭābī: among humans, one charged with guarding, vigilant;
  **murāqaba** = the servant's continuing knowledge that God watches him, "the key of all goods". (7) **al-Muhaymin** (Q59:23): knowledge of
  a thing's states, complete power to secure its welfare, and constancy in doing so. ★ **"When we reached this place, something occurred
  that compelled cutting off this kind of discourse."** [Autobiographical interruption: the names catalogue is left unfinished.]
- *Two closing inquiries*: (1) God's power in creating genera, species, kinds and individuals, in essences, attributes and the wisdom of
  their making, is nearly infinite; hence a special name for each, and **whoever knows more of creation's divisions and the traces of wisdom
  has more knowledge of God's names: "this is the secret and the mainstay of this chapter"**. (2) **Incantations and spells (ruqā, ʿazāʾim)
  in unintelligible words** are names of God, of angels or of devils; God's names are noblest and strongest in effect; since names reduce to
  these divisions, the unknown words must ultimately signify these attributes. ★ Razi's conjecture on why they work: **the reciter, not
  understanding them, is seized by fear and awe, and at that moment the rational soul's substance connects with the world of the separate
  and holy beings, so its effects grow stronger.**

### L11934– Bāb 9 F6 God as wise (ḥakīm)
(Orig. F18.) Wisdom is (1) *theoretical* (knowing realities; His knowledge is most perfect, so His wisdom is) and (2) *practical*: acts
leading to benefit or away from harm. For oneself = **motive of appetite (dāʿī al-shahwa)**, impossible in God; for others = **motive of
wisdom (dāʿī al-ḥikma)**. Two routes to proving He has the latter, philosophical and Muʿtazilī, "each in its best form".
- *Philosophers' route* (the Avicennian theodicy): things are pure good; mostly good with some evil; equal; mostly evil; or pure evil. Pure
  good must be created; mostly good too, since **"abandoning much good for a little evil is much evil"** and the good may be rationally
  inseparable from that little evil (so "why not create it without the evil?" fails); weighing the two evils, bearing the lesser is better.
  Equal is not required; the last two are against wisdom. Creatures are pure goods or preponderantly good: the hungry are many but the sated
  more, likewise the sick vs the healthy, the defective vs the sound → all creation is wise. **Good is willed and approved; evil is willed and
  disliked: disliked in itself, willed by accident and consequence** as a concomitant of the good. Verse of "the wisest of poets": grey hair
  is hated, yet hated too is parting [from life]; how strange a thing loved despite hatred. Grey hair is disliked in itself, but life is
  impossible without it, so it is loved by accident.
- ★ **Razi's comments, first point**: "why did this agent do good / evil?" makes sense only for a **free chooser**; for a necessitating
  cause the question drops. Its knowing what proceeds from it does not make the question proper without choice: **a rational man who falls
  from a roof onto another and kills him cannot properly be asked "why did you kill him?"**. Since **the philosophers' First is a mūjib
  bi-l-dhāt, this whole inquiry does not fit their system; it fits only those who hold God a free agent** …
